## Summary

*The Chronicles of Narnia: The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* was released on December 9, 2005, becoming one of the year's most significant cinematic events and a major commercial blockbuster. Directed by Andrew Adamson (*Shrek*), the film adapted C.S. Lewis's beloved 1950 fantasy novel for the big screen, bringing the magical world of Narnia to contemporary audiences through state-of-the-art computer-generated imagery and practical effects. The film starred William Moseley and Anna Popplewell as eldest siblings Peter and Susan Pevensie, with Georgie Henley and Skandar Keynes as Lucy and Edmund. Tilda Swinton delivered an iconic performance as the White Witch Jadis, and Liam Neeson voiced the lion Aslan. James McAvoy played Mr. Tumnus, the faun who first befriends Lucy in Narnia.

The film's release came on the heels of the massive success of *The Lord of the Rings* trilogy (2001–2003), which had demonstrated the commercial viability of large-scale fantasy adaptations. Produced jointly by Walden Media and Walt Disney Pictures on a budget of approximately $180 million, it grossed over $291 million domestically and approximately $745 million worldwide, making it one of 2005's highest-grossing films and establishing Narnia as a viable franchise. Principal photography took place across New Zealand, the Czech Republic, and Poland, with visual effects supervised by Dean Wright and Jim Berney.

## Production

Pre-production began in late 2004, with Adamson focusing on fidelity to Lewis's original text while modernizing the visual presentation. The film's CGI work, produced by Rhythm & Hues Studios, rendered Aslan and hundreds of fantastical creatures—centaurs, minotaurs, dwarfs—for the climactic Battle of Beruna. Practical sets, animatronics, and a 500-person costume department contributed to the film's scale. The film earned a PG rating from the MPAA for "battle sequences and frightening moments." It received an Academy Award nomination for **Best Makeup** (2006), recognizing the work on Tumnus, the centaurs, and the White Witch's pallid appearance.

## Critical Reception

Critics praised the film's faithfulness to the source material and its technical achievements, though some noted uneven pacing. On Rotten Tomatoes the film earned approximately a **75% approval rating**, certified Fresh, with critics citing the performances of Swinton and Henley as highlights. Roger Ebert awarded the film 3.5 out of 4 stars, praising its "technical splendor and the emotional power that the story has always possessed." The film's Christian allegorical undertones—Aslan as a Christ figure—were both celebrated by religious audiences and examined critically by reviewers.

## Significance

*The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* marked a turning point in fantasy filmmaking, demonstrating that classic children's literature could attract audiences comparable to adult-oriented blockbusters. The film initiated a Narnia franchise: *Prince Caspian* followed in 2008 and *The Voyage of the Dawn Treader* in 2010 (distributed by Fox after Disney's exit). Its box-office success accelerated studio investment in literary fantasy properties through the late 2000s, contributing to adaptations such as *The Golden Compass* (2007) and *Percy Jackson & the Olympians* (2010). The film's digital-creature pipeline built on techniques pioneered by *The Lord of the Rings* and pushed motion-capture performance toward greater naturalism in the lead-up to productions like *Avatar* (2009).

The 2005 release also competed directly with other major prestige titles: it entered theaters one week before *King Kong* (December 14) and finished the year alongside the acclaimed drama [*Brokeback Mountain*]({{ '/news/arts-culture/brokeback-mountain/' | relative_url }}), which dominated awards conversations, and earlier in the year alongside the sci-fi blockbuster [*Star Wars: Episode III – Revenge of the Sith*]({{ '/news/arts-culture/star-wars-episode-iii/' | relative_url }}), which had opened in May.
