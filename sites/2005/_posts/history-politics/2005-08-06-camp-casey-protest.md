**Key figures**: Cindy Sheehan, Casey Sheehan (U.S. Army Specialist, killed April 4, 2004, Sadr City, Iraq), President George W. Bush, Gold Star Families for Peace.

## Background

By the summer of 2005, the Iraq War had entered its third year with no clear exit strategy in sight. U.S. troop fatalities had passed 1,800, and a Gallup poll published in August 2005 found that 54 percent of Americans believed the invasion had been a mistake — the first time a majority held that view. The war had been a defining issue in the 2004 presidential election, yet Bush's re-election did little to quiet dissent; anti-war groups were looking for a galvanizing moment and a compelling human voice.

Cindy Sheehan, from Vacaville, California, had met briefly with President Bush at Fort Lewis, Washington, in June 2004 — two months after her son Casey was killed in an ambush in Sadr City. She later described that meeting as unsatisfying and felt Bush had been dismissive. When Bush retreated to his Prairie Chapel Ranch near Crawford, Texas, for a five-week summer vacation in early August 2005, Sheehan saw an opportunity.

## Chronology of the Vigil

**August 6** — Sheehan and a small group of supporters arrived at a ditch along Prairie Chapel Road, roughly 1.5 miles from the ranch gate. She requested a meeting with Bush and announced she would remain until he agreed. White House officials declined, and Deputy Chief of Staff Joe Hagin and National Security Adviser Steve Hadley came out to speak with her briefly on the first day.

**August 7–11** — National media descended on Crawford. MSNBC, CNN, and Fox News aired live coverage. Dozens of supporters joined; the encampment acquired tents, portable toilets, and a kitchen serving donated food. Sympathizers from across the country began driving and flying to Texas. Veterans for Peace and Military Families Speak Out, two prominent anti-war organizations, sent delegations.

**August 12** — Volunteers erected nearly 1,000 white crosses at the site, modeled on the Arlington West memorial installations held in California by Veterans for Peace. The visual of rows of crosses — each bearing the name of a fallen U.S. service member — provided powerful imagery for news broadcasts worldwide.

**August 13** — A local resident, Larry Mattlage, fired a shotgun into the air near the camp, citing frustration with the disruption to his property. No one was injured. The incident drew additional national attention.

**August 14** — A pro-Bush counter-demonstration organized by a local supporter used pickup trucks to drag a trailer carrying a large American flag through crosses representing the war dead, knocking several down. Anti-war demonstrators rebuilt them. Separate counter-protest gatherings eventually drew up to 4,000 participants on some days.

**August 18** — Sheehan temporarily left Camp Casey after her mother, Shirley Miller, suffered a stroke. She returned days later, with the camp continuing in her absence under the management of co-organizers.

**August 25–27** — At its peak, Camp Casey hosted more than 700 people at once. Celebrities including Joan Baez, Steve Earle, and members of the band R.E.M. performed or appeared in solidarity. Democratic members of Congress including Senators Barbara Boxer and John Kerry visited.

**August 30** — President Bush cut short his vacation by two days and returned to Washington to oversee the federal response to Hurricane Katrina, which made landfall that morning. The encampment formally dispersed shortly thereafter, though Sheehan had already announced plans to extend the protest to Washington, D.C.

## Significance

Camp Casey reinvigorated the American anti-war movement at a moment of broad public skepticism about the war's rationale. It achieved several concrete effects:

- **Media agenda shift**: National television coverage of a single grieving mother proved more viscerally effective than large-scale marches. Networks that had given limited space to anti-war voices provided sustained coverage across the full 26-day vigil.
- **Grassroots replication**: Hundreds of "Camp Casey" solidarity encampments appeared in cities across the United States and in several other countries, demonstrating that anti-war sentiment had organized, geographically distributed support.
- **Political pressure**: The protest contributed to a summer of difficult polling for the Bush administration. By September 2005, Bush's approval rating had fallen to 45 percent in a CBS News/New York Times poll, down from 51 percent at his January 2005 second inauguration.
- **Gold Star Families movement**: The camp formalized the role of bereaved military families as a distinct anti-war constituency, giving organizations like Gold Star Families for Peace national visibility. This voice — unlike that of traditional peace activists — was politically difficult to dismiss as unpatriotic.

The protest must be read against the wider political landscape of 2005: [Bush's second-term agenda](/news/history-politics/george-w-bush-2005-agenda/) had launched with ambitious domestic and foreign policy goals in January, but the G8 summit at Gleneagles in July had revealed widening international divergence on Iraq (see [G8 Gleneagles Summit](/news/history-politics/g8-gleneagles-summit/)), and the [July 7 London bombings](/news/history-politics/77-london-bombings/) had re-sharpened debate over whether the "War on Terror" was making Western countries safer. The opening of [Saddam Hussein's trial](/news/history-politics/saddam-hussein-trial-begins/) in October 2005 offered the administration a counter-narrative of justice and progress, but Camp Casey had already established a durable moral question in the public mind: what was the noble cause?

## Legacy

Sheehan continued anti-war activism for years after 2005, though her later actions generated controversy within both anti-war circles and the broader public. Camp Casey itself — the physical site — became a recurring gathering point for protests during subsequent Bush vacations in Crawford. The name entered the political lexicon as shorthand for citizen confrontation of executive authority on war policy.
