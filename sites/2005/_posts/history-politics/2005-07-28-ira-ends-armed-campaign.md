## Summary

On 28 July 2005 the Provisional Irish Republican Army (IRA) issued a statement formally ordering an end to its armed campaign, which had lasted since 1969. Effective from 4:00 p.m. British Summer Time that day, the leadership instructed all IRA units to "dump arms" and directed volunteers to pursue republican objectives exclusively through peaceful and democratic means. The organisation said it had authorised its representative to work with the Independent International Commission on Decommissioning (IICD) to put its weapons verifiably beyond use.

The announcement was among the most significant milestones in the Northern Ireland peace process since the 1998 Good Friday Agreement. Two months later, on 26 September 2005, General John de Chastelain, chairman of the IICD, confirmed that the IRA had decommissioned its entire arsenal — a process witnessed by two independent clergymen, the Catholic priest Father Alec Reid and the Methodist minister Reverend Harold Good.

## Background

The statement followed years of stalled negotiations and a series of controversies that had eroded confidence in the republican movement. In December 2004 the £26.5 million Northern Bank robbery in Belfast was blamed by police on the IRA, and in January 2005 the murder of Robert McCartney in a Belfast bar — and the intimidation of witnesses that followed — provoked widespread condemnation, including from within nationalist communities. These events increased pressure on Sinn Féin and the IRA from the British and Irish governments and from the United States to demonstrate a definitive break with paramilitary activity.

The IRA said its decision was reached after an "unprecedented internal discussion and consultation process" with its units and volunteers. Sinn Féin president Gerry Adams had publicly appealed to the IRA in April 2005 to embrace exclusively peaceful methods, and the July statement was widely read as the organisation's formal response.

## The Statement

The core of the 28 July 2005 statement declared that the leadership had "formally ordered an end to the armed campaign" and instructed all volunteers "to assist the development of purely political and democratic programmes through exclusively peaceful means." Volunteers were told not to engage in "any other activities whatsoever" beyond politics. The IRA framed the move as a way to advance its objectives while removing the justification for continued armed conflict.

British Prime Minister Tony Blair called the statement a step of "unparalleled magnitude," and Irish Taoiseach Bertie Ahern welcomed it while stressing that words would need to be matched by verifiable action.

## Decommissioning

Acting on the statement, the IRA conducted a series of decommissioning operations across Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland over the following weeks. On 26 September 2005 General de Chastelain announced that the IICD was satisfied the IRA had put its entire stock of arms beyond use, with most of the destruction having taken place in the preceding week. Rather than accept photographs of the process — which the IRA rejected as an attempt at humiliation — the commission arranged for two independent church witnesses to observe. Father Alec Reid and Reverend Harold Good publicly attested that the decommissioning was complete.

## Significance

The end of the IRA's armed campaign removed one of the central obstacles to a durable political settlement in Northern Ireland. It cleared the way for the restoration of devolved power-sharing government, ultimately realised in the 2007 St Andrews Agreement, under which Sinn Féin and the Democratic Unionist Party entered government together. The 2005 statement and the subsequent verified decommissioning marked the effective conclusion of the Troubles as an armed conflict, a period of some three decades that had claimed roughly 3,500 lives. It stands as a landmark example of a paramilitary organisation formally abandoning violence in favour of constitutional politics.
