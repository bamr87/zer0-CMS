**Key figures**: Rafik Hariri (former Prime Minister of Lebanon), Bashar al-Assad (President of Syria), Omar Karami (pro-Syrian Prime Minister), Salim Ayyash (convicted in absentia)

## Summary

On 14 February 2005, Rafik Hariri, the billionaire businessman who had served as Lebanon's prime minister for most of the post-civil-war period, was killed by a massive truck-bomb explosion as his motorcade passed the seafront St. George Hotel in Beirut. The blast — equivalent to roughly 1,000 kilograms of TNT — killed Hariri and 21 other people and wounded more than 200. It was the most consequential political assassination in Lebanon since the civil war.

The killing ignited a wave of mass protests known as the Cedar Revolution, or Independence Intifada, that demanded the withdrawal of Syrian forces from Lebanon and an end to nearly three decades of Syrian domination of Lebanese politics. The largely peaceful, civil-resistance movement reached its climax on 14 March 2005, when more than a million Lebanese — by some estimates roughly a quarter of the country's population — rallied in central Beirut.

Under sustained domestic and international pressure, Syria withdrew the last of its approximately 14,000 troops on 26 April 2005, ending a military presence that had lasted 29 years. The assassination also prompted a United Nations investigation that eventually led to the creation of the Special Tribunal for Lebanon.

## The Assassination

Hariri had resigned as prime minister in October 2004 amid friction with Damascus over the Syrian-backed extension of President Émile Lahoud's term, a move he had opposed. On the morning of 14 February 2005 his motorcade was struck near the St. George Hotel on Beirut's Mediterranean corniche by explosives detonated as the vehicles passed. Among the dead was the former economy minister Bassel Fleihan; several of Hariri's bodyguards were also killed.

The attack came less than six months after the adoption of United Nations Security Council Resolution 1559 on 2 September 2004, sponsored by France and the United States, which had called for the withdrawal of all remaining foreign forces from Lebanon and the disbanding of militias. Many Lebanese held Syria and its allies responsible for Hariri's death, an accusation Damascus denied.

## The Cedar Revolution

In the weeks after the assassination, large crowds gathered in Beirut's Martyrs' Square demanding the truth about the killing and the departure of Syrian troops. The Syrian-backed government of Prime Minister Omar Karami resigned in late February under the pressure of the demonstrations, though Karami was briefly reappointed before Najib Mikati formed a caretaker cabinet.

Lebanon's political forces polarised into two camps that took their names from rival rallies. On 8 March 2005 a large demonstration organised by Hezbollah expressed gratitude to Syria, giving rise to the "March 8" alliance. In response, on 14 March an opposition rally drew a crowd estimated at over a million people demanding full Syrian withdrawal — the largest demonstration in the country's history and the origin of the "March 14" alliance. The movement was notable for its overwhelmingly peaceful character and reliance on civil resistance.

## Syrian Withdrawal

President Bashar al-Assad announced on 5 March 2005 that Syria would bring its forces home, and the withdrawal proceeded over roughly seven weeks. On 26 April 2005 the last Syrian troops crossed back into Syria, ending a military presence in Lebanon that dated to 1976, during the Lebanese Civil War. The departure fulfilled a central demand of Resolution 1559 and of the protest movement.

## Investigation and Tribunal

The United Nations responded by establishing an international inquiry. Security Council Resolution 1595, adopted on 7 April 2005, created the UN International Independent Investigation Commission to assist Lebanese authorities. The investigation ultimately led the Security Council to establish the Special Tribunal for Lebanon, a hybrid court seated near The Hague. On 18 August 2020 the tribunal convicted Salim Ayyash, identified as a member of Hezbollah, in absentia of involvement in the attack that killed Hariri and 21 others and injured 226; it found no evidence that the leadership of Hezbollah or Syria had ordered the assassination, and acquitted three co-defendants. Ayyash was sentenced in December 2020 to five concurrent terms of life imprisonment.

## Significance

The assassination of Rafik Hariri was a turning point in modern Middle Eastern politics. It triggered a popular movement that, within ten weeks, ended a 29-year Syrian military presence in Lebanon and reordered the country's political landscape into the rival March 8 and March 14 blocs that would dominate Lebanese politics for years. The Cedar Revolution was widely cited alongside other mid-decade movements as part of a broader regional moment of demands for democratic change, and the international investigation it set in motion produced one of the few internationally backed tribunals to address a political assassination. The unresolved tensions it exposed — over Syrian influence, the role of Hezbollah, and Lebanon's sectarian balance — continued to shape the country long after 2005.
