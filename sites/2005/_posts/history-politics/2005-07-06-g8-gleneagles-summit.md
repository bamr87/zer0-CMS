## Summary

The 31st G8 summit was held from July 6 to 8, 2005 at the Gleneagles Hotel in Auchterarder, Perthshire — the first G8 summit hosted in Scotland. As holder of the rotating presidency of the Group of Eight, British Prime Minister Tony Blair chaired the meeting and set its two headline priorities: economic development in Africa and global climate change.

On development, the leaders committed to increase official development assistance to poor countries by around $50 billion a year by 2010, of which roughly $25 billion a year would go to Africa — effectively a doubling of aid to the continent. The summit also endorsed the cancellation of some $40 billion in multilateral debt owed by 18 heavily indebted poor countries — 14 of them in Africa — to the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank, and the African Development Bank, building on a debt deal reached by G8 finance ministers weeks earlier. A further group of countries was identified as eligible for relief on a further $15 billion of debt upon meeting governance conditions.

The summit was overshadowed by the [7/7 London bombings](/news/history-politics/77-london-bombings/), which struck the capital on the morning of July 7, the meeting's second day. Blair briefly left Gleneagles to return to London, leaving Foreign Secretary Jack Straw to deputise, before returning to conclude the summit. It was also the focus of an unprecedented mobilisation by anti-poverty campaigners, coordinated with the [Live 8 concerts](/news/arts-culture/live-8-concerts/) and the Make Poverty History coalition.

## The Africa and Development Agenda

Blair had made Africa a signature cause of Britain's 2005 G8 presidency, drawing on the recommendations of his Commission for Africa, which had reported in March 2005. The commission called for large increases in aid, debt cancellation, and fairer trade terms for African economies. The Gleneagles communiqué translated much of this ambition into headline pledges:

- **Aid**: an increase in annual development assistance of about **$50 billion by 2010**, roughly half of it directed to Africa.
- **Debt**: full cancellation of the multilateral debt of **18 qualifying heavily indebted poor countries**, covering a debt stock of about **$40 billion**, with a further set of countries eligible for around **$15 billion** more if they met conditions on governance and anti-corruption.
- **Health and governance**: commitments toward universal access to HIV/AIDS treatment by 2010, support for a new African peacekeeping capacity, and matching pledges by African leaders to democratic and transparent governance.

The debt element built directly on an agreement reached by G8 finance ministers in London on June 11, 2005, which the Gleneagles leaders formally endorsed and expanded.

## Climate Change

Climate change, Blair's second priority, produced a markedly weaker outcome. The United States, under President George W. Bush, had withdrawn from the Kyoto Protocol in 2001 and continued to reject binding emissions targets and timetables. The final communiqué acknowledged that human activity contributes to global warming and committed the G8 to a "dialogue" on clean-energy technology, but it set no binding limits. Environmental groups criticised the result as a retreat from meaningful action, with critics noting that the summit produced no new targets. The compromise language reflected the difficulty of reconciling the European members' support for the [Kyoto framework](/news/science-technology/kyoto-protocol-enters-force/), which had entered into force in February 2005, with US opposition.

## Live 8, Make Poverty History, and Public Pressure

The summit was preceded and accompanied by one of the largest coordinated public campaigns ever aimed at a G8 meeting. On July 2, 2005, the [Live 8 concerts](/news/arts-culture/live-8-concerts/) — ten simultaneous benefit shows organised by Bob Geldof and Midge Ure across the G8 nations and South Africa — drew a global broadcast audience explicitly to press the Gleneagles leaders on African poverty and debt. The same day, the Make Poverty History coalition staged a march in Edinburgh that drew an estimated 200,000–250,000 participants, many wearing the campaign's white wristbands.

The activism deliberately fused mass entertainment with policy demands, framing the summit as a test of whether public mobilisation could move the G8 toward concrete commitments on aid and debt. Campaigners subsequently offered mixed verdicts: the debt and aid pledges were real and substantial, but later monitoring found that several of the aid commitments were only partially met by the 2010 deadline.

## Protests and Security

Gleneagles saw a large security operation, with more than 10,000 police officers drawn from forces across the United Kingdom deployed around Auchterarder and Edinburgh. Anti-globalisation and anti-capitalist demonstrations accompanied the summit; on July 6, a group of several hundred protesters breached a section of the perimeter fence near the hotel before being turned back by police. Over the course of the mobilisation around the summit, roughly 700 people were arrested and several hundred charged.

## The 7 July Bombings

On the morning of July 7, 2005, four coordinated suicide bombings on London's transport network killed 52 people and injured hundreds. Blair, addressing reporters at Gleneagles alongside the other leaders, condemned the attacks and travelled to London to coordinate the government's response before returning to complete the summit later the same day. The G8 leaders issued a joint statement condemning the bombings. The attacks dominated global coverage of the summit's second and third days and became inextricably linked to the memory of Gleneagles.

## Significance

Gleneagles is remembered as the high-water mark of the mid-2000s "Make Poverty History" moment, when debt cancellation and aid to Africa briefly reached the top of the international agenda. Its debt-relief and aid pledges were among the most concrete development commitments in G8 history, and the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative that followed did cancel tens of billions of dollars of debt for the poorest countries. At the same time, the summit exposed the limits of the format: the aid promises were only partly delivered by 2010, and the failure to secure binding climate commitments underscored the divide between the United States and its partners in the years before the 2015 Paris Agreement.

The summit's coincidence with the 7/7 bombings also marked a symbolic collision of the era's two dominant themes — globalised humanitarian ambition and transnational terrorism — within a single 48-hour period, ensuring that Gleneagles occupies a distinctive place in the history of 2005.
