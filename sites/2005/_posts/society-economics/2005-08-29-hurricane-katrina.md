## Summary

Hurricane Katrina formed over the Bahamas on August 23, 2005, crossed southern Florida as a Category 1 storm on August 25, then intensified rapidly over the exceptionally warm waters of the Gulf of Mexico, reaching Category 5 status on August 28 with peak sustained winds near 175 miles per hour (280 km/h) and a minimum central pressure of 902 millibars. The storm weakened slightly before making its second and decisive landfall near Buras-Triumph, Louisiana, at approximately 6:10 AM CDT on August 29, 2005, as a Category 3 hurricane with sustained winds of about 125 mph.

The storm drove a catastrophic storm surge — reaching up to 28 feet (8.5 m) along portions of the Mississippi Gulf Coast — into Louisiana, Mississippi, and Alabama. Mississippi's Harrison and Hancock counties were virtually obliterated at the waterline; Biloxi, Gulfport, and Pass Christian sustained near-total destruction of coastal infrastructure.

### The Flood of New Orleans

The greatest catastrophe unfolded in New Orleans, where the federally built levee and floodwall system failed in more than 50 locations within hours of landfall. The most consequential breaches occurred on the 17th Street Canal and the London Avenue Canal, where I-wall sections collapsed due to flawed design; the floodwalls had been built without accounting for weak soil layers beneath their foundations. These failures were later characterized by independent investigators as engineering failures, not acts of nature. Floodwaters inundated roughly 80 percent of the city — much of which lies 1 to 3 meters below sea level — with depths reaching up to 4.5 meters (15 feet) in some Lower Ninth Ward neighborhoods.

Tens of thousands of residents who had not or could not evacuate sought refuge at the Louisiana Superdome, which sheltered an estimated 26,000 people by August 29, and at the Ernest N. Morial Convention Center, where an additional 19,000 gathered without pre-positioning of supplies. For several days, conditions in both facilities deteriorated sharply as food, water, and medical care ran critically short. National Guard troops and federal responders did not arrive in force until September 2–3, roughly 72–96 hours after landfall.

### Casualties and Damage

The official death toll reached 1,836, making Katrina the deadliest U.S. hurricane since the 1928 Okeechobee hurricane. Louisiana recorded approximately 1,577 deaths; Mississippi, 238. An estimated $125 billion in direct damage was recorded at the time (later revised to approximately $161 billion in 2023 dollars), establishing Katrina as the costliest natural disaster in U.S. history at the time of occurrence.

## Federal Response and Criticism

The federal response was coordinated through the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) under Director Michael Brown, who had limited emergency management experience before his appointment. Critical decisions — including the delay in federalizing the National Guard and the failure to preposition rescue assets — were later scrutinized in congressional hearings. Brown resigned on September 12, 2005, twelve days after landfall, following intense public criticism.

A bipartisan U.S. House Select Committee released its report, *A Failure of Initiative*, in February 2006, concluding that failures at every level of government — federal, state, and local — had compounded the disaster. The U.S. Senate Homeland Security Committee issued a parallel report, *Hurricane Katrina: A Nation Still Unprepared*, in May 2006. Both found systemic failures in communications interoperability, command structure, and pre-storm evacuation planning. The Post-Katrina Emergency Management Reform Act, signed by President Bush in October 2006, reorganized FEMA within the Department of Homeland Security and required comprehensive pre-disaster planning protocols.

The Army Corps of Engineers commissioned an independent forensic investigation (the Interagency Performance Evaluation Task Force, IPET). Its 2006 report acknowledged that the levee system's failure was a result of "a system that was in many cases built to less than design strength." A separate independent levee investigation team (ILIT) used even stronger language, calling the failures "a catastrophic failure of the national engineering and regulatory system."

## Displacement and Demographic Change

The storm displaced an estimated 400,000 residents from the New Orleans metropolitan area alone, and over one million people from the broader Gulf Coast. Unlike most hurricane evacuees, a large fraction never returned: New Orleans's population fell from approximately 484,000 before the storm to roughly 208,000 by January 2006. By the 2010 census the city had recovered to about 343,000, still nearly 30 percent below its pre-storm level. The diaspora scattered primarily to Houston, Baton Rouge, Atlanta, and Dallas, permanently changing the demographic and cultural composition of those receiving cities.

The disaster's uneven toll on African American, low-income, and elderly residents — who were disproportionately represented among those who could not evacuate and among the dead — generated sustained national debate over structural inequality and the relationship between poverty, race, and vulnerability to catastrophe.

## Long-Term Recovery and Policy Legacy

Less than four weeks after Katrina, [Hurricane Rita]({{ '/news/society-economics/hurricane-rita-2005/' | relative_url }}) made landfall on the Louisiana–Texas border on September 24, 2005, overtopping recently patched levees and reflooding parts of New Orleans and complicating early recovery. Reconstruction of New Orleans's levee system, funded by the federal government at a cost exceeding $14.5 billion, was declared complete in June 2011. The rebuilt system provides protection against a 100-year storm, compared with the pre-Katrina system rated for a moderate hurricane. The Road Home program, administered by Louisiana, distributed approximately $8.1 billion in grants to homeowners, though implementation was plagued by delays and inconsistencies.

Katrina reshaped national emergency management doctrine, accelerating moves toward pre-positioned supplies, interoperable communications, and formal mutual-aid compacts between states. Its legacy also extended to climate policy debates: the correlation between Gulf of Mexico sea-surface temperatures and storm intensity intensified scientific and political discussions about hurricane climatology and global warming, contributing context to the concurrent entry into force of the [Kyoto Protocol]({{ '/news/science-technology/kyoto-protocol-enters-force/' | relative_url }}) in February 2005.

## Significance

Hurricane Katrina ranks among the deadliest and most destructive natural disasters in U.S. history. It exposed critical failures in flood-control engineering, emergency preparedness, and intergovernmental coordination; catalyzed major reforms to FEMA and national emergency management law; and reignited a sustained national reckoning with poverty, race, and the geography of vulnerability. Its aftermath permanently reshaped New Orleans demographically, spurred a multi-decade infrastructure investment, and influenced disaster-response planning globally.
