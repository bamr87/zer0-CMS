## Summary

On April 27, 2005, the first Airbus A380 — the world's largest passenger airliner — made its maiden flight from Toulouse–Blagnac Airport in France. The prototype, flight-test aircraft MSN001 registered F-WWOW, took off at about 10:30 local time with a crew of six and landed after roughly 3 hours and 54 minutes, having completed the primary items on its first flight-test programme. Chief test pilot Jacques Rosay described handling the double-deck airliner as "like handling a bicycle," a remark that came to symbolise how manageable the aircraft felt despite its unprecedented size. The flight opened a flight-test and certification campaign for the four-engined "superjumbo" that Airbus had unveiled at a rollout ceremony in Toulouse three months earlier, on January 18, 2005.

## Development Background

Airbus formally launched the A380 programme — originally designated the A3XX during design studies — on December 19, 2000, with a projected development cost of €9.5 billion. Conceived as a direct challenge to the Boeing 747's decades-long monopoly on the very-large-aircraft market, the A380 was designed as a full-length double-deck airliner seating around 525 passengers in a typical three-class layout and certified for as many as 853 in an all-economy configuration. Its size required new manufacturing and logistics arrangements, with major sections built across Airbus sites in France, Germany, Spain, and the United Kingdom and transported by ship, barge, and road to the final assembly line in Toulouse.

The prototype was publicly unveiled at a "reveal" ceremony in Toulouse on January 18, 2005, attended by the political leaders of the four partner nations. By 2005 the programme's estimated development cost had risen to roughly €10.3 billion, and the aircraft's complexity — particularly its wiring — would later contribute to significant production delays. The first prototype was powered by four Rolls-Royce Trent 900 engines; a General Electric/Pratt & Whitney alliance's Engine Alliance GP7200 was offered as an alternative powerplant.

## The First Flight

The maiden flight was conducted with water ballast aboard to simulate passenger and cargo loads and to allow the crew to test stability across the aircraft's centre-of-gravity range. Co-captains Jacques Rosay and Claude Lelaie flew the aircraft alongside flight-test engineer Fernando Alonso and additional test personnel, working through a checklist of primary flight-test objectives covering the aircraft's basic handling, systems, and low-speed behaviour. The uneventful, successful flight was watched by tens of thousands of spectators around the airport and marked the start of a multi-aircraft test campaign aimed at certification.

Following the first flight, Airbus flew a fleet of test aircraft through a demanding programme of route-proving, cold- and hot-weather trials, and structural testing. The A380 received joint type certification from the European Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) and the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) on December 12, 2006. Production difficulties delayed the first delivery: Singapore Airlines took delivery of the first commercial A380 on October 15, 2007, and operated its inaugural revenue service on October 25, 2007.

## Significance

The A380's first flight was a landmark in commercial aviation, demonstrating that a full-length double-deck airliner could be built and flown and giving Airbus a flagship at the top of the airliner market. For a time the aircraft reshaped long-haul travel around a "hub-and-spoke" vision of very large jets linking major airports, and it became a symbol of European industrial collaboration.

The commercial story proved more difficult than the technical one. Airlines increasingly favoured smaller, more fuel-efficient twin-engined widebodies flown on point-to-point routes over concentrating traffic through mega-hubs, and the A380 never recovered its enormous development costs. Airbus announced in 2019 that it would end A380 production, delivering the final aircraft in 2021. The programme is thus remembered both as an extraordinary engineering achievement — the largest passenger aircraft ever to enter service — and as a cautionary study in how market forecasts can diverge from the realities of airline economics.
