**Key figures:** Steve Jobs (Apple CEO), Jon Rubinstein (SVP Hardware Engineering), Tony Fadell (VP iPod Division), Jonathan Ive (SVP Industrial Design)

## Summary

Apple unveiled the first-generation iPod Nano on **September 7, 2005**, at a special event in San Jose, California, with units reaching retail within days and broad availability continuing through the weeks that followed. Steve Jobs introduced the Nano by pulling it out of the small coin pocket in his jeans, a theatrical gesture that immediately communicated the device's most striking attribute: it was barely larger than a stick of gum.

### Hardware Specifications

The first-generation iPod Nano (model A1137) used **NAND flash memory** rather than the spinning hard disk drives found in the iPod mini and standard iPod—a fundamental architectural shift that enabled its unprecedented thinness. Key specifications:

| Specification | Details |
|---|---|
| Dimensions | 3.5 × 1.6 × 0.27 in (88.9 × 40.6 × 6.9 mm) |
| Weight | 1.5 oz (42 g) |
| Display | 1.5-inch 176 × 132 color LCD |
| Storage | 1 GB (240 songs), 2 GB (500 songs), 4 GB (1,000 songs) |
| Battery life | Up to 14 hours audio playback |
| Pricing | $149 (1 GB), $199 (2 GB), $249 (4 GB) |
| Colors | White, Black |
| Connectivity | USB 2.0, dock connector |

The Nano replaced the iPod mini, which had been Apple's best-selling iPod model. Discontinuing a product at the height of its commercial success—the mini had sold over 5 million units—to introduce a successor was characteristic of Jobs's strategy of deliberate self-disruption.

### The Event

The September 7 announcement event took place at the California Theatre in San Jose. Jobs also announced the iTunes 5 update and the iTunes Music Store's video content expansion at the same event, including music videos and short films. The Nano launch was accompanied by the tagline *"1,000 songs in your pocket"*—a callback to the original iPod's 2001 slogan—updated to the corollary: *"The iPod mini is gone. Nanos are here."*

## Significance

### Sales and Commercial Impact

The iPod Nano sold **14 million units in its first fiscal quarter** (Q1 FY2006, ending December 2005), exceeding analyst expectations and contributing to Apple reporting its most profitable quarter in company history at that time. The device's success helped push Apple's overall iPod sales to **32.6 million units for the 2005 calendar year**—a **61% year-over-year increase**.

Apple's stock price rose substantially through the period following the Nano's launch, reflecting investor confidence in Apple's product pipeline and its control of the digital music ecosystem through the combination of iTunes, the iTunes Store (which had surpassed **500 million song downloads** by July 2005), and the iPod hardware family.

### The Scratch Controversy

Within weeks of launch, widespread complaints emerged that the Nano's polycarbonate screen was unusually prone to scratching under normal use—even from keys in a pocket. Apple's initial response was measured, attributing damage to misuse; the company later added a free protective sleeve to retail packaging and adjusted the supply chain. The controversy, while significant, did little lasting damage to sales, but contributed to the subsequent generation's adoption of scratch-resistant glass. The episode was an early high-profile example of how social media and internet forums could amplify product defect reporting at a scale previously impossible.

### Flash Memory and the Music Industry

The Nano's use of NAND flash was a pivot point for the consumer electronics industry. Flash-based storage at the scale and price point the Nano required had not previously been economically viable for mainstream consumer devices; Apple's purchasing agreements, reportedly securing a large portion of global NAND flash supply in 2005, locked competitors out of comparable component sourcing for 12–18 months. This supply chain strategy, combined with the Nano's design, gave Apple a temporary monopoly on thin, flash-based music players at mass-market prices.

The device's success also reinforced the record industry's embrace of digital download retail. The iTunes Store had launched in April 2003; by the time the Nano launched in late 2005, digital sales were moving from novelty to a meaningful revenue stream, and the Nano's sales directly correlated with iTunes Store usage growth.

### Design Legacy

Jonathan Ive's industrial design team produced a device that has been widely cited as among the best examples of consumer electronics design from the 2000s. The 1st-generation Nano's profile—a thin rectangular slab with a click wheel—became the visual archetype for the entire mobile device design era that followed. The design's influence is visible in every subsequent thin-slab smartphone, from the iPhone onward.

The iPod Nano line continued through **seven generations** until Apple discontinued it in **July 2017**, twelve years after its introduction. Total lifetime sales across all generations reached the tens of millions.

## Competitive Context in 2005

The Nano arrived in a digital music player market that Apple already dominated. Key competitors included:
- **Creative Zen Micro** — a 5 GB hard-drive player emphasizing battery life and FM radio
- **iRiver H10** — available in 5 and 6 GB variants with photo display
- **Sony Network Walkman NW-E507** — flash-based, but limited to 512 MB

None could match the Nano's combination of storage, size, and design at comparable price points. The Nano's release effectively ended serious competition for Apple in the flash player segment until the smartphone era.
