## Summary

In December 2005, the South Korean scientist Hwang Woo-suk — a veterinary cloning researcher at Seoul National University (SNU) hailed at home as a national hero and "Supreme Scientist" — was revealed to have fabricated the data underlying his most celebrated results. Hwang had claimed a series of breakthroughs in somatic-cell nuclear transfer (SCNT), the cloning technique used to create embryos genetically matched to a patient, from which embryonic stem cells could in principle be derived for regenerative therapies.

Two papers in the journal *Science* had made Hwang's reputation. A 2004 paper reported the first derivation of a human embryonic stem cell line from a cloned blastocyst. A second paper, published on June 17, 2005, claimed that his team had produced eleven patient-specific embryonic stem cell lines from 185 donated eggs — a result greeted worldwide as a major step toward personalized cell therapies. By the end of 2005 both claims had collapsed under investigation, and both papers were retracted.

## The Unravelling

The scandal broke in two stages. On November 22, 2005, the investigative program *PD Notebook* on South Korea's Munhwa Broadcasting Corporation (MBC) aired an exposé raising the possibility of ethical violations in how Hwang's laboratory had obtained human eggs, including donations from junior researchers in his own lab and payments to donors. Hwang initially denied wrongdoing but soon acknowledged that members of his team had provided eggs and stepped down from some official positions. The broadcaster itself faced a public backlash over its aggressive reporting methods, and for a time domestic sympathy remained with Hwang.

Attention then shifted from ethics to the integrity of the science itself. On December 15, 2005, Hwang's collaborator Roh Sung-il publicly alleged that nine of the eleven claimed stem cell lines had been faked and said Hwang had admitted to fabrication; Hwang asked *Science* to withdraw the 2005 paper. SNU convened an investigation panel, which announced on December 29, 2005, that the 2005 results were fabricated and that no patient-matched embryonic stem cells existed. In its final report on January 10, 2006, the panel concluded that both the 2004 and 2005 papers had been fabricated. *Science* unconditionally retracted both papers on January 11, 2006.

## Aftermath

Seoul National University dismissed Hwang from his professorship on March 20, 2006. He was later indicted on charges of fraud, embezzlement of research funds, and violations of South Korea's bioethics law governing egg procurement. On October 26, 2009, a Seoul court convicted him of embezzlement and of bioethics violations related to the purchase of human eggs, while acquitting him of the central fraud charge; he received a two-year suspended prison sentence. Hwang subsequently continued to work in animal cloning at a private research foundation.

Not all of Hwang's work proved false. Snuppy, an Afghan hound produced by his laboratory and announced in August 2005 as the first cloned dog, was independently verified by the investigation panel — through DNA analysis of genetic markers and mitochondrial sequencing — to be a genuine somatic-cell clone. It remained the laboratory's one confirmed landmark achievement.

## Significance

The Hwang affair was among the most consequential scientific frauds of its era. It embarrassed a leading peer-reviewed journal, exposed weaknesses in the verification of high-profile results, and set back public confidence in therapeutic-cloning research at a moment of intense expectation. In South Korea, where Hwang had been a source of national pride and substantial state funding, the collapse prompted soul-searching about the pressures of "science nationalism." Internationally, the case sharpened debate over research-integrity oversight, the ethics of egg donation, and the responsibilities of co-authors and journals — themes that ran alongside other 2005 milestones in biomedicine and bioethics such as the [world's first partial face transplant]({{ '/news/science-technology/first-face-transplant/' | relative_url }}).
