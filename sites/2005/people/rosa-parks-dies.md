# Rosa Parks Dies

**Category**: People  
**Key figures**: Rosa Parks, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., Congressman John Conyers Jr., Raymond Parks

## Summary

Rosa Louise McCauley Parks died on October 24, 2005, at age 92 in her Detroit apartment, after a years-long decline attributed to progressive dementia diagnosed in 2004. Born February 4, 1913, in Tuskegee, Alabama, Parks joined the Montgomery chapter of the NAACP in the early 1940s, becoming its secretary — a role that placed her at the intersection of organized civil rights strategy when her defining moment arrived.

### The 1955 Act of Resistance

On December 1, 1955, Parks refused to surrender her seat in the "colored" section of a Montgomery city bus to a white passenger after the white section filled, violating city segregation ordinances. She was arrested and charged. Though later mythologized as purely spontaneous, her act grew from years of organized resistance: Parks had attended a civil rights training workshop at the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee just months earlier, and colleagues within the NAACP recognized her arrest as strategically sound for a legal challenge.

### The Montgomery Bus Boycott

Her arrest triggered the Montgomery Bus Boycott, which ran 381 days — from December 5, 1955, to December 20, 1956. An estimated 40,000 Black commuters participated, walking miles or carpooling rather than riding segregated buses, and inflicting significant financial damage on the city's transit system. The U.S. Supreme Court effectively ended the boycott on November 13, 1956, when it upheld a federal district court ruling in *Browder v. Gayle* that bus segregation was unconstitutional. The boycott launched Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. — then 26, newly arrived as pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church — to national and international prominence as a civil rights leader.

### Life After Montgomery

Parks and her husband Raymond faced sustained threats and economic retaliation in Montgomery. Unable to find steady work, they relocated to Detroit in 1957. From 1965 to 1988, she served on the congressional staff of U.S. Representative John Conyers Jr. (D-MI), handling constituent casework and community outreach. In 1987, she co-founded the Rosa and Raymond Parks Institute for Self-Development, dedicated to providing educational programs for youth. She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1996 and the Congressional Gold Medal in 1999 — the two highest civilian honors in the United States.

### Death and National Mourning

Following her death on October 24, 2005, Parks received honors typically reserved for elected officials and military figures. Her casket lay in honor at the U.S. Capitol Rotunda from October 30 to 31, 2005 — making her the first woman, and the second African American, ever accorded this distinction. An estimated 50,000 people filed past her casket in Washington. Her funeral was held at Greater Grace Temple in Detroit on November 2, 2005, attended by thousands including President George W. Bush and First Lady Laura Bush. She was later interred at Detroit's Woodlawn Cemetery.

## Significance

Rosa Parks' 1955 act catalyzed the desegregation of Montgomery's public transit system and furnished the civil rights movement with one of its most resonant symbols of dignified defiance. The national mourning in October–November 2005 — including the unprecedented honor of lying in state at the Capitol — reflected her transcendence from regional activist to international icon. Her decades of work in Detroit after 1957, often less visible than the 1955 moment, demonstrated that her commitment to justice was not a single act but a sustained vocation. The arc of her life, from Tuskegee to the Capitol Rotunda, illustrated how individual conscience, embedded in collective organizing, could reshape law and culture across generations.

Parks' death in 2005 came the same year the world also mourned [Pope John Paul II](../history-politics/pope-john-paul-ii-death.md), another figure whose moral authority had shaped the late 20th century; both deaths prompted unusually large public expressions of grief and gratitude.
