# Michael Jackson trial verdict

**People**

**Key figures:** Michael Jackson (defendant), Judge Rodney Melville, prosecutor Tom Sneddon (Santa Barbara County District Attorney), defense attorneys Thomas Mesereau Jr. and Susan Yu, Gavin Arvizo (accuser), jury foreperson Paul Rodriguez

## Summary

Michael Jackson was acquitted on June 13, 2005, of all fourteen charges in one of the most heavily covered celebrity trials in American history. The verdict came after a five-month trial at the Santa Barbara County courthouse in Santa Maria, California, capping a legal saga that had begun with Jackson's arrest in November 2003.

### Background and charges

The prosecution centered on Gavin Arvizo, a 13-year-old cancer survivor who had appeared in a Martin Bashir documentary about Jackson's Neverland Ranch in February 2003. Santa Barbara County District Attorney Tom Sneddon, who had investigated Jackson over unproven allegations in 1993, filed a 10-count indictment in April 2004. The charges included four counts of lewd and lascivious acts with a minor under 14, one count of attempted lewd act, four counts of administering an intoxicating agent (alcohol) to a minor, and one count of conspiracy covering extortion, child abduction, and false imprisonment of the Arvizo family.

### Trial proceedings (January–June 2005)

Jury selection concluded January 28, 2005, and opening statements began on January 31. The prosecution called 82 witnesses over roughly ten weeks, presenting testimony about events at Neverland in February and March 2003. Defense attorney Thomas Mesereau mounted an aggressive cross-examination strategy, attacking the credibility of the Arvizo family and presenting witnesses who testified that Gavin had made similar accusations in the past. Jackson attended all 61 days of testimony — sometimes visibly unwell, arriving in pajama bottoms on one occasion after being hospitalized for a back injury.

The jury of eight women and four men received the case on June 3, 2005, and deliberated for approximately 32 hours across seven days. On June 13 they returned unanimous verdicts of not guilty on all 10 original counts and all four lesser-included charges.

### Aftermath

Jackson emerged from the courthouse to cheers from thousands of supporters who had camped outside throughout the trial. He subsequently traveled to Bahrain as a guest of Sheikh Abdullah, effectively beginning a period of self-imposed exile from the United States that lasted until 2006. Santa Barbara County had spent an estimated $3.5 million prosecuting the case. Jackson's defense was reported to have cost more than $5 million.

## Significance

The acquittal was a landmark in celebrity jurisprudence. District Attorney Sneddon had pursued Jackson publicly for over a decade, and the not-guilty verdict was widely interpreted as a rebuke of what critics called an overzealous prosecution. At the same time, the trial permanently damaged Jackson's commercial standing: album sales and licensing deals had slowed dramatically since his 2003 arrest, and the reputational cost outlasted the legal one.

The case sharpened debate about media saturation of high-profile trials — court television networks devoted near-continuous coverage — and about whether celebrity defendants could receive fair proceedings. Jury foreperson Paul Rodriguez said afterward that the jurors found the Arvizo family's testimony inconsistent and not credible beyond a reasonable doubt, illustrating how prosecutorial credibility is often the decisive variable in complex child-abuse cases.

Jackson's death on June 25, 2009, reignited public interest in the allegations. The 2019 HBO documentary *Leaving Neverland*, featuring two men who claimed Jackson had abused them as children in earlier years, prompted a new wave of debate that continues to shape how his musical legacy is assessed — demonstrating that a not-guilty verdict does not permanently settle public opinion.
