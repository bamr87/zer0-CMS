# Lance Armstrong wins seventh Tour de France

**Category**: People
**Key figures**: Lance Armstrong, Ivan Basso (2nd place), Jan Ullrich (3rd place), George Hincapie, Levi Leipheimer, Johan Bruyneel (team director)

## Summary

Lance Armstrong won his seventh consecutive Tour de France on July 24, 2005, crossing the finish line on the Champs-Élysées with a total race time of **86 hours, 15 minutes, 2 seconds** — more than **4 minutes and 40 seconds** ahead of second-place finisher Ivan Basso (Team CSC) and nearly 6 minutes ahead of third-place Jan Ullrich (T-Mobile). The 2005 Tour ran from July 2 to July 24 over 3,608 kilometers (2,242 miles) divided into 21 stages, including grueling alpine sections in the Pyrenees and Alps. Armstrong's Discovery Channel team, directed by Belgian coach Johan Bruyneel, executed a near-flawless tactical campaign, protecting their leader through the mountain stages while launching decisive attacks when rivals were most vulnerable.

Born Lance Edward Gunderson on September 18, 1971, in Plano, Texas (later adopting the surname of his stepfather Terry Armstrong), Armstrong had been diagnosed with advanced testicular cancer in 1996 — a cancer that had spread to his brain, lungs, and abdomen. His remission and return to elite cycling made him one of sports' most prominent comeback narratives. In 2005, at age 33, he became the oldest Tour de France winner since the 1920s.

## The 2005 Tour: Race Narrative

Armstrong entered the 2005 Tour as a heavy favorite but faced pressure from Basso and a resurgent Ullrich. He did not win the prologue (that went to David Zabriskie) but took the yellow jersey after Stage 4 and never fully relinquished overall dominance. Key moments included:

- **Stage 10 (Courchevel)**: Armstrong cracked Basso on the final climb, opening a gap that proved decisive for overall classification.
- **Stage 11 (Briançon to Courchevel)**: Armstrong attacked solo on the final ascent, winning the stage and extending his lead to over 2 minutes over Basso.
- **Stage 20 (Time Trial, St-Étienne)**: Armstrong won the final individual time trial, adding further seconds to his lead and effectively sealing the overall victory.

He stood atop the podium at the traditional Champs-Élysées finish in Paris and **announced his retirement from professional cycling** at the post-race ceremony — a declaration he would eventually reverse in 2009 when he returned to race again.

## The Livestrong Foundation

The 2005 victory amplified the profile of Armstrong's cancer awareness charity, the **Lance Armstrong Foundation** (later rebranded Livestrong in 2012). Yellow Livestrong silicone wristbands — first produced in 2004 for the Athens Olympics — had by mid-2005 become a global phenomenon, with over **70 million** wristbands sold. Proceeds funded cancer research programs and patient navigation services. The wristband popularized a broader trend of charity silicone bands across sports and cause marketing. Armstrong's celebrity allowed Livestrong to expand its reach into schools, hospitals, and international health organizations during 2005.

## Significance and Later Controversy

Armstrong's seventh Tour victory made him a transcendent global sports figure whose story of illness and triumph resonated far beyond cycling audiences. His achievement attracted mainstream media coverage, celebrity sponsorships, and a White House visit. However, the win occurred against an increasingly turbulent backdrop: the French sports newspaper *L'Équipe* published a front-page investigation in August 2005 claiming that stored urine samples from Armstrong's 1999 Tour de France victory had tested positive for EPO using a new detection method — allegations Armstrong denied.

The full reckoning came in October 2012, when the **U.S. Anti-Doping Agency (USADA)** released a 1,000-page report concluding that Armstrong had orchestrated "the most sophisticated, professionalized and successful doping program that sport has ever seen." The International Cycling Union subsequently stripped Armstrong of all seven Tour titles, leaving the 2005 edition with no official winner. Armstrong publicly admitted to doping in a January 2013 television interview with Oprah Winfrey.

The rise and fall of Armstrong's story shaped how sports media and anti-doping authorities approached athlete testing and narrative accountability for a generation. In 2005, though, he remained the celebrated face of cycling and a global symbol of athletic resilience — a cultural context shared with major events of that year including [Hurricane Katrina's](../society-economics/hurricane-katrina.md) test of American fortitude and the confirmed achievement of [Condoleezza Rice](condoleezza-rice-secretary-of-state.md) as Secretary of State, which together defined 2005 as a year of both landmark success stories and impending reckonings.
