# Condoleezza Rice becomes U.S. Secretary of State

**Category**: People
**Key figures**: Condoleezza Rice, George W. Bush, Colin Powell, Senator Barbara Boxer (lead opponent during confirmation)

## Summary

Condoleezza Rice was confirmed as the 66th U.S. Secretary of State on January 26, 2005, succeeding Colin Powell in President George W. Bush's second term. The Senate voted 85–13 in her favor, making it the most opposition votes cast against a Secretary of State nominee in decades. Born on November 14, 1954, in Birmingham, Alabama, Rice became the first African American woman to hold the position — the second African American (after Powell) and the second woman (after Madeleine Albright, 1997–2001). Her appointment marked a significant milestone in American diplomatic history.

Rice had served as National Security Advisor since January 2001, guiding Bush's foreign policy through the September 11 attacks and the subsequent invasions of Afghanistan and Iraq. Before joining the White House, she had been Provost of Stanford University (1993–1999) and a Professor of Political Science there, specializing in Soviet and Russian foreign and security policy. Her academic background shaped her approach to alliance management and great-power competition during her tenure at State.

## Background and Career Path

Rice earned a bachelor's degree in political science from the University of Denver in 1974, a master's from Notre Dame in 1975, and a PhD from Denver's Graduate School of International Studies in 1981 — focusing on the Soviet military and Czechoslovakia. She joined the National Security Council staff in 1989 under Brent Scowcroft during the George H.W. Bush administration, coordinating policy on the Soviet Union as communism collapsed. She returned to Stanford, then joined the 2000 Bush presidential campaign as a key foreign policy advisor.

## Confirmation and Early Tenure

The January 18–19, 2005 confirmation hearings before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee were contentious. Senator Barbara Boxer (D-CA) issued the sharpest challenge, accusing Rice of "contradictions" and a "credibility gap" over statements made about Iraq's weapons of mass destruction and the decision to invade. Despite opposition, the committee voted 16–2 to recommend her confirmation, with the full Senate confirming her 85–13 on January 26.

Rice's early agenda as Secretary centered on repairing alliances strained by the Iraq War, particularly with France and Germany, which had opposed the 2003 invasion. Her first overseas trip in February 2005 included stops in London, Paris, Berlin, Warsaw, Ankara, Jerusalem, and Ramallah — a fourteen-day diplomatic blitz signaling that multilateral engagement would define her tenure. She also pushed for European unity on Iran's nuclear program, helping to establish the EU3+US diplomatic framework that would evolve into the broader P5+1 talks.

## Significance

Rice's appointment represented a watershed in African American representation at the highest levels of American government. She served until January 20, 2009 — nearly a full four-year term — making her one of the longer-serving Secretaries of State of the early 21st century. Her tenure encompassed the [January 2005 Iraqi elections](../history-politics/iraqi-elections-2005.md), the Cedar Revolution in Lebanon, North Korea nuclear negotiations, and the beginning of Israeli-Palestinian peace efforts. Her confirmation occurred in a year of intense scrutiny of U.S. foreign policy and American identity, the same year [Rosa Parks died](rosa-parks-dies.md) (October 24, 2005), connecting Rice's milestone to a longer arc of African American advancement in public life.

After leaving office, Rice returned to Stanford as a professor and senior fellow at the Hoover Institution. She also served on the Board of Directors of Chevron Corporation, Transamerica Corporation, and Hewlett-Packard.
