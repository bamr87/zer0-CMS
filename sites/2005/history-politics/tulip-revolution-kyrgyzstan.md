# Tulip Revolution in Kyrgyzstan

**Category**: History & Politics
**Key figures**: Askar Akayev (ousted President of Kyrgyzstan), Kurmanbek Bakiyev (opposition leader and interim head of state), Felix Kulov (opposition figure released during the uprising), Roza Otunbayeva (interim foreign minister)

## Summary

The Tulip Revolution was a wave of popular protest in the spring of 2005 that toppled President Askar Akayev, who had governed Kyrgyzstan since the country's independence from the Soviet Union in 1991. The unrest was triggered by parliamentary elections held on 27 February and 13 March 2005 that opposition groups and international monitors — notably the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) — condemned as marred by fraud, alongside long-standing grievances over corruption and authoritarian rule by Akayev's family and allies.

Demonstrations began in the southern cities of Jalal-Abad and Osh before spreading north to the capital, Bishkek. On 24 March 2005 protesters stormed the main government headquarters, known as the White House, and Akayev fled the country the same day, travelling first to Kazakhstan and then to Russia. Three people died in the unrest, and mobs looted stores and cash machines in Bishkek as the government collapsed.

An interim administration led by the opposition figure Kurmanbek Bakiyev took power, naming him acting president and prime minister. Akayev signed a formal resignation at the Kyrgyz embassy in Moscow on 4 April 2005, and the interim parliament accepted it on 11 April. In a presidential election on 10 July 2005, Bakiyev won by a landslide, taking roughly 89 percent of the vote.

## Background

Askar Akayev, a physicist who had led Kyrgyzstan since 1990, was once regarded as the most reform-minded leader in post-Soviet Central Asia, but by 2005 his rule was widely associated with corruption, nepotism, and the concentration of economic power within his family. The parliamentary elections of February and March 2005 became the immediate flashpoint: opposition candidates were disqualified or defeated in contests that observers judged neither free nor fair, and several members of Akayev's family secured seats. The pattern echoed the disputed votes that had preceded earlier post-Soviet uprisings.

## The Uprising

Protests first gathered force in the southern strongholds of Jalal-Abad and Osh in mid-March, where demonstrators seized regional administrative buildings. The opposition leader Kurmanbek Bakiyev, head of the People's Movement of Kyrgyzstan, aligned himself with the protesters. On 24 March 2005 the demonstrations reached Bishkek, and crowds overran the presidential and government complex. Akayev and Prime Minister Nikolay Tanayev fled rather than order a violent crackdown, and the seat of government fell with little resistance. In the confusion, looting broke out across the capital and three people were killed. The imprisoned opposition figure Felix Kulov was freed and briefly placed in charge of security, while Roza Otunbayeva took over as interim foreign minister.

## Aftermath

The interim leadership designated Bakiyev acting prime minister and then acting head of state pending a new election. Akayev negotiated the terms of his departure from Moscow, signing his resignation on 4 April 2005; it was ratified by the interim parliament on 11 April. Bakiyev consolidated his position by agreeing to run for president with Kulov as his prime minister, and on 10 July 2005 he won the presidency with about 89 percent of the vote. The alliance and the political settlement it produced proved unstable, and Kyrgyzstan experienced renewed upheaval later in the decade, including a second revolution that removed Bakiyev himself in 2010.

## Significance

The Tulip Revolution was the third of the so-called "colour revolutions" that reshaped the post-Soviet space in the mid-2000s, following Georgia's Rose Revolution of 2003 and Ukraine's Orange Revolution of 2004. It was the first such movement to unseat a government in Central Asia, a region otherwise dominated by entrenched authoritarian rulers, and it demonstrated that mass mobilisation over a disputed election could bring down a long-serving president. Unlike the largely disciplined demonstrations in Tbilisi and Kyiv, the Kyrgyz revolt was marked by looting and disorder, and its outcome — a swift transfer of power to a new leader who soon faced the same accusations of corruption and authoritarianism — tempered contemporary hopes that it heralded durable democratisation. The events nonetheless established a pattern of street-led political change in Kyrgyzstan that recurred in 2010 and 2020.
