# George W. Bush's 2005 Second-Term Agenda

**Category**: History & Politics  
**Key figures**: George W. Bush (President), Dick Cheney (Vice President), Karl Rove (Deputy Chief of Staff), Condoleezza Rice (Secretary of State), Donald Rumsfeld (Secretary of Defense), John Roberts (Supreme Court Chief Justice nominee), Samuel Alito (Supreme Court nominee), Harriet Miers (withdrawn Supreme Court nominee), Michael Brown (FEMA Director), Alberto Gonzales (Attorney General)

## Summary

President George W. Bush's second term, which began January 20, 2005, opened with an ambitious domestic agenda centered on Social Security privatization, tax permanence, and judicial reform. The year marked a sharp inflection point in his presidency: his domestic initiatives collapsed by autumn, particularly after Hurricane Katrina exposed federal preparedness failures and eroded public confidence in his administration. Yet 2005 proved his most consequential year for long-term institutional change, with the successful confirmation of Supreme Court Chief Justice John Roberts (September 29) and the initiation of the nominating process that would place Samuel Alito on the Court by January 2006. The formalization of Iraq War strategy in late 2005 and the steepening decline in presidential approval ratings set the trajectory for the remainder of his tenure.

## Inauguration and State of the Union

Bush's second inaugural address on January 20, 2005, delivered on the West Front of the U.S. Capitol before an estimated audience of 100,000, was among the most explicitly ideological in American history. Drafted primarily by Michael Gerson, the 21-minute speech used the words "freedom," "free," and "liberty" some 49 times combined, laying out a foreign policy doctrine that the United States would work to "end tyranny in our world." The address framed the global struggle against terrorism as a generational democratic mission, committing the United States to supporting democratic movements worldwide as a matter of national security. Critics from both parties noted the speech's ambitious scope; it was interpreted by some analysts as a declaration of democratic interventionism that broadened the Bush Doctrine beyond Afghanistan and Iraq.

On February 2, Bush delivered his State of the Union address to a joint session of Congress, outlining his second-term legislative priorities. His domestic agenda centered on three pillars: reform of Social Security through personal retirement accounts, making permanent the 2001 and 2003 tax cuts due to expire in 2010, and reducing the federal deficit by cutting discretionary spending. He also called for immigration reform, tort reform, and renewal of the USA PATRIOT Act. Bush's approval rating at this point stood at approximately 51%, reflecting the narrow 50.7%–48.3% popular-vote margin of his November 2004 re-election victory over Senator John Kerry.

## Social Security Campaign

Beginning in January 2005, Bush launched a 60-day national "conversation tour" on Social Security reform — the most extensive domestic policy campaign of his presidency. The proposal would allow workers under 55 to divert up to 4% of their payroll tax contributions (subject to a ceiling of $1,000 annually) into personal investment accounts holding a mix of stocks and bonds. Bush held more than 60 town-hall meetings in 25 states between February and May 2005 to build public support.

The campaign failed comprehensively. Opinion polling documented the erosion: a January 2005 Gallup poll showed 49% opposing and 43% supporting personal accounts; by May, opposition had risen to 64%. Congressional Republicans, many facing 2006 mid-term elections in competitive districts, distanced themselves from the proposal. Senate Majority Leader Bill Frist and House Speaker Dennis Hastert never brought the plan to a floor vote. After Katrina struck in late August, the Social Security initiative was permanently shelved — no Congressional committee ever produced a bill embodying Bush's proposal, and the administration never formally returned to it.

The failure was structural as well as political: the Congressional Budget Office estimated that funding the transition costs of private accounts would require borrowing $1–2 trillion over the next decade, a figure that undermined Bush's parallel commitment to deficit reduction.

## Supreme Court Reconstruction

Bush's most consequential domestic achievement in 2005 came through the federal judiciary. In a series of moves from July to October, he initiated a fundamental reshaping of the Supreme Court:

- **July 1**: Justice Sandra Day O'Connor announced her retirement, effective upon confirmation of a successor. O'Connor had been the Court's decisive swing vote for two decades.
- **July 19**: Bush nominated federal appeals court judge John G. Roberts Jr. for O'Connor's seat on the Court.
- **September 3**: Chief Justice William Rehnquist died, having served since 1972 (Chief Justice since 1986). Bush withdrew Roberts's nomination to the associate seat and re-nominated him as Chief Justice.
- **September 29**: The Senate confirmed Roberts as the 17th Chief Justice of the United States by a vote of 78–22. At age 50, Roberts was the youngest person to assume the role since John Jay in 1789 and the first new Chief Justice in 19 years.
- **October 3**: Bush nominated White House Counsel Harriet Miers for O'Connor's former seat. Conservatives objected that she lacked judicial experience and had no established constitutional philosophy; liberals questioned her qualifications.
- **October 27**: Miers withdrew her nomination after 24 days, citing Senate demands for White House documents she and Bush argued were protected by executive privilege.
- **October 31**: Bush nominated federal appeals court judge Samuel A. Alito Jr. Alito was confirmed January 31, 2006, by a vote of 58–42.

Together the Roberts and Alito confirmations constituted the most significant ideological shift in the Supreme Court since the 1980s. O'Connor, a Republican moderate who had provided the fifth vote for abortion rights, affirmative action, and campaign finance regulation in major cases, was replaced by Alito, a consistent conservative. This reshaping of the Court would prove durable for decades, with the Roberts Court ultimately overturning *Roe v. Wade* in 2022 in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*.

## Hurricane Katrina and Political Crisis

Hurricane Katrina made landfall near Buras, Louisiana, at 6:10 AM CDT on August 29, 2005, as a Category 3 storm with peak sustained winds of 125 mph. Storm surge overwhelmed the New Orleans levee system, flooding approximately 80% of the city. The catastrophe was both meteorological and institutional: the levees, maintained by the Army Corps of Engineers, were later found to have failed in part due to design flaws.

The federal response became a political crisis of the first order. Bush remained at his Crawford, Texas, ranch through August 30; on August 31, an aerial photograph of him gazing at the flooding from Air Force One — published under the headline "Out of Touch" by multiple newspapers — became one of the defining images of his presidency. He did not visit New Orleans until September 2, four days after landfall.

FEMA, led by Michael Brown — a political appointee with no prior emergency management experience — struggled to coordinate relief efforts. Brown's famous exchange with Bush, in which the President said "Brownie, you're doing a heck of a job" on September 2, became a symbol of disconnected federal management. Brown resigned under pressure on September 12.

Bush's response on September 15, an address to the nation from a flood-lit Jackson Square in New Orleans, acknowledged federal responsibility and pledged $200 billion in reconstruction funds. The federal government ultimately spent approximately $120 billion on recovery. However, the political damage was irreversible: Bush's approval rating fell from approximately 50% in August to 39% by October, and polling showed a collapse in public confidence in federal competence that would not recover during his tenure. For more on the disaster's scale, see the [Hurricane Katrina](../society-economics/hurricane-katrina.md) article.

## Iraq War and Foreign Policy

The year 2005 brought significant milestones and deepening challenges in Iraq. On January 30, Iraq held its first multi-party legislative elections since 1953, with approximately 8.5 million Iraqis voting (about 58% turnout) despite insurgent threats and 26 polling-station attacks (see [Iraqi Parliamentary Elections](iraqi-elections-2005.md)). The elections produced a transitional assembly dominated by Shia religious parties, beginning the political process the Bush administration hoped would lead to a stable democratic Iraq.

By mid-year the insurgency had intensified. Suicide bombings, improvised explosive device attacks, and sectarian violence were killing an average of 60–100 Iraqis per week. Over 2,000 U.S. military personnel had died in Iraq by October 26, 2005, when the 2,000th death was recorded — a milestone that prompted renewed debate about the war's cost and strategy.

On November 30, the White House released the **"National Strategy for Victory in Iraq"** — a 35-page unclassified document that articulated a three-track approach:
1. **Political**: achieving Iraqi constitutional ratification (accomplished October 15, 2005, with 63% approval), holding parliamentary elections (December 15, 2005), and forming an inclusive government.
2. **Security**: training Iraqi security forces (the Iraqi Army numbered approximately 90,000 trained personnel by year's end, up from 5,000 in 2004) and transitioning lead responsibility from U.S. to Iraqi forces.
3. **Economic**: reconstruction projects, oil infrastructure repair, and international support.

The document represented an acknowledgment that the conflict would require a sustained, multi-year commitment — a significant departure from the pre-invasion expectation of a short war.

On the broader foreign policy front, Bush appointed Condoleezza Rice as Secretary of State on January 26, replacing Colin Powell. Rice's confirmation on January 26 (85–13 in the Senate) marked a major shift in the administration's diplomatic posture; she was the first African American woman to hold the office (see [Condoleezza Rice becomes Secretary of State](../people/condoleezza-rice-secretary-of-state.md)). Rice's tenure emphasized the "transformational diplomacy" framework, seeking to engage non-state actors and civil society alongside governments. The administration also monitored the July 7 London bombings closely as a validation of its counter-terrorism strategy and maintained pressure on Syria following the [Hariri assassination and Cedar Revolution](hariri-assassination-cedar-revolution.md) in Lebanon.

## Energy Policy Act

Signed August 8, 2005, the Energy Policy Act of 2005 (Public Law 109-58) was the first major federal energy legislation in more than a decade. Key provisions included:
- **$14.5 billion in tax incentives** for energy production across fossil fuels, nuclear power, and renewables.
- A **Renewable Fuel Standard** requiring 7.5 billion gallons of ethanol blended into gasoline by 2012.
- Extended daylight saving time by four weeks, beginning in 2007.
- Streamlined Nuclear Regulatory Commission licensing processes for new nuclear plants, the first such reform in 25 years.

Bush framed the legislation as advancing energy independence and reducing reliance on foreign oil. Critics noted the act heavily favored traditional energy industries over conservation and efficiency measures.

## Congressional Relations and Domestic Defeats

Beyond Social Security, Bush's second-term domestic agenda suffered additional setbacks in 2005. A proposal for comprehensive immigration reform — including a path to legal status for approximately 11 million undocumented immigrants — failed to move through Congress due to conservative resistance in the House. The USA PATRIOT Act, due to expire December 31, 2005, required an emergency extension after the Senate blocked reauthorization; it was not permanently renewed until March 2006. Conservative allies also pressed for a constitutional amendment to ban same-sex marriage — the Federal Marriage Amendment, reintroduced in the Senate in early 2005 — but it never reached a floor vote that year; a cloture motion on the measure would ultimately fail 49–48 in June 2006, keeping the debate in state legislatures.

The 109th Congress, Republican-controlled in both chambers, nonetheless passed the Class Action Fairness Act (February 18) and the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act (April 20), two of Bush's legislative priorities from his first term.

## Approval Rating Trajectory

| Period | Approval Rating (Gallup) | Context |
|--------|--------------------------|---------|
| January 2005 | ~51% | Inauguration; re-election mandate |
| March 2005 | ~52% | Social Security campaign underway |
| June 2005 | ~45% | Social Security failing; Iraq casualties rising |
| September 2005 | ~40% | Post-Katrina collapse |
| November 2005 | ~37% | Iraq strategy document, Miers withdrawal |
| December 2005 | ~38% | Iraqi elections, modest stabilization |

The September decline of approximately 10 percentage points over six weeks was one of the steepest sustained drops for a second-term president in modern polling history. Bush's approval ratings would not exceed 45% again for the remainder of his presidency.

## Significance

2005 represents the pivotal year in which George W. Bush's presidency fundamentally changed character. He began the year with broad political capital and an explicit mandate for domestic reform — yet by autumn, his approval ratings had collapsed by 10–15 percentage points, his domestic agenda lay in ruins, and his political focus was consumed by war, disaster response, and the confirmation battles that would reshape the judiciary. The loss of public confidence after Katrina and the escalating costs and casualties of the Iraq War shifted the presidency from a period of ideological offensive to one of defensive management.

Paradoxically, 2005 was also the year in which Bush's longest-lasting institutional legacy was secured. The confirmation of John Roberts and the nomination of Samuel Alito shifted the Supreme Court's ideological center at a pace that would define American jurisprudence for decades — arguably more consequential than any legislation passed or defeated in 2005. The year thus encapsulates the contradictory arcs of Bush's presidency: political decline in the near term, judicial dominance in the long term.

The year also marked the acceleration of a geopolitical realignment that would define the late 2000s. The rise of Angela Merkel as German Chancellor in September 2005 (see [Angela Merkel elected Chancellor](angela-merkel-chancellor.md)) began a new era in transatlantic relations, with a more pragmatic European partner than the Schröder government had been. And the continuing insurgency in Iraq set the conditions for the 2006 "troop surge" debate that would dominate Bush's final two years.
