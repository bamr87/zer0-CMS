# Terri Schiavo case

**Society & Economics**

**Key figures:** Theresa Marie "Terri" Schiavo (patient), Michael Schiavo (husband and legal guardian), Robert and Mary Schindler (parents), Judge George Greer (Pinellas County Circuit Court), Governor Jeb Bush (Florida), President George W. Bush, Senator Bill Frist, Representative Tom DeLay, Dr. Ronald Cranford (neurologist, court-appointed)

## Summary

Theresa Marie Schiavo died on March 31, 2005, at the age of 41, thirteen days after her feeding tube was removed for the third and final time under a Pinellas County court order. Her death ended a fifteen-year legal dispute that had drawn the United States Congress and President George W. Bush into what courts and ethicists widely regarded as a private end-of-life medical decision — producing one of the most divisive episodes in American bioethics history.

### Background

Born December 3, 1963, Terri Schiavo suffered cardiac arrest at her home in St. Petersburg, Florida, on February 25, 1990, at age 26. Oxygen deprivation caused severe and irreversible brain damage. She never regained consciousness. Neurologists who examined her, including the court-appointed Dr. Ronald Cranford, diagnosed her with a persistent vegetative state (PVS) — a condition characterized by preserved sleep–wake cycles but absent awareness, cognition, or voluntary movement. Her husband Michael Schiavo, who served as her legal guardian, petitioned Pinellas County Circuit Court in May 1998 to withdraw the feeding tube, stating that Terri had told him she would not want to be kept alive artificially. Her parents, Robert and Mary Schindler, disputed both the diagnosis and Michael's account of her wishes, and mounted continuous legal opposition.

### Prolonged litigation (1998–2005)

Judge George Greer became the primary decision-maker after years of evidentiary hearings. He ruled repeatedly that Terri was in a PVS and that Michael Schiavo had credibly established her wish not to be kept alive with artificial life support. The feeding tube was removed three times over the course of the litigation: first on April 24, 2001 (reinserted two days later after an appeal), a second time on October 15, 2003, and finally on March 18, 2005.

After the second removal, the Florida Legislature passed "Terri's Law" on October 21, 2003, authorizing Governor Jeb Bush to order reinsertion of the tube. The Florida Supreme Court unanimously struck down the law as an unconstitutional violation of separation of powers on September 23, 2004. The U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal.

### Federal intervention (March 2005)

The final withdrawal on March 18, 2005, triggered extraordinary congressional action. Working through the night of March 20–21, Congress passed "An Act for the Relief of the Parents of Theresa Marie Schiavo," a private bill granting federal courts jurisdiction to hear a new challenge by the Schindlers. President George W. Bush returned from his ranch in Crawford, Texas, and signed the bill at 1:11 a.m. EST on March 21, 2005. The Schindlers immediately filed in U.S. District Court in Tampa. Judges at the district level and the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals declined to order reinsertion; the U.S. Supreme Court refused to intervene on March 24. All subsequent emergency motions were denied.

Terri Schiavo died on March 31, 2005, at the Woodside Hospice in Pinellas Park, Florida. An autopsy conducted by Pinellas-Pasco County Medical Examiner Jon Thogmartin found her brain weighed approximately half the expected weight for a woman her age, with damage consistent with PVS and inconsistent with recovery — definitively contradicting claims made by some advocacy groups that she had been misdiagnosed.

## Significance

The Schiavo case became the defining American bioethics controversy of the early 21st century, crystallizing tensions that earlier cases such as *In re Quinlan* (1976) and *Cruzan v. Director* (1990) had raised but not resolved in the public mind.

### Legal and medical legacy

The case accelerated the adoption of advance directives — legal documents such as living wills and healthcare proxies — across the United States. Healthcare providers and advocacy organizations reported a surge in demand for directive forms in April 2005. Several states subsequently tightened statutory requirements for withdrawing life support, while others clarified procedures for surrogate decision-making. The autopsy's findings reinforced professional medical consensus on PVS diagnosis and gave courts a clearer evidentiary basis for future cases.

### Political dimensions

Congressional intervention was widely criticized by legal scholars as an encroachment on judicial independence and state jurisdiction. Polling conducted by ABC News during the final week of March 2005 found that 70 percent of Americans disapproved of Congress passing legislation in the case, and 63 percent said the president should not have signed it. The episode is often cited in retrospectives on the Republican Party's 2005–2006 political trajectory, as the widely perceived overreach contributed to declining approval ratings for Congress and the administration.

### Disability rights and cultural debate

Disability rights organizations were divided. Some argued that removal of the feeding tube reflected societal undervaluation of disabled lives; others contended that Terri's autonomy and expressed wishes deserved respect. The case forced a broader cultural reckoning with what constitutes a dignified death, how families navigate profound disagreements about a loved one's care, and whether the state has a legitimate interest in overriding individual and surrogate medical decisions.
