# iTunes Digital Music Revolution

**Category**: Society & Economics
**Key figures**: Steve Jobs (Apple CEO), Eddy Cue (Apple VP Internet Services), Jimmy Iovine (Interscope/Universal), the Big Four labels (Universal Music Group, Sony BMG, EMI, Warner Music Group)

## Summary

The year 2005 marked the inflection point at which digital music sales crossed from novelty to economic necessity for the recorded music industry. Global digital music revenues nearly tripled from approximately $400 million in 2004 to $1.1 billion, while digital single-track downloads reached 470 million units. Apple's iTunes Music Store, launched in April 2003, commanded an estimated 80% of the legal digital download market by 2005, making Apple the world's largest music retailer despite its relatively late entry into retail as a business. Digital downloads' share of recorded music revenue grew from 0.9% in 2004 to 5.7% in 2005, signaling the beginning of the physical media era's decline and the restructuring of one of the world's oldest creative industries.

## Market Data

The RIAA (Recording Industry Association of America) reported that U.S. shipment value of CDs fell 8.28% in 2005, with CD market share dropping from 90.3% to 87.0% year-over-year. Globally, the International Federation of the Phonographic Industry (IFPI) tracked digital music revenues as a discrete category for the first time in its 2006 annual report, underscoring how significant the market had become.

Key 2005 benchmarks:
- **470 million** single-track digital downloads sold globally
- **$1.1 billion** in global digital music revenue (up from ~$400 million in 2004)
- **80% market share** held by Apple's iTunes Store among legal digital download platforms
- **5.7%** of total recorded music revenue — up from 0.9% in 2004
- **$0.99 per track** — the iTunes price point that set the industry standard for individual song pricing

## Apple's Dominance

iTunes' dominance rested on several simultaneous advantages. The platform's seamless integration with the iPod, which had sold more than 22 million units by the end of 2005, created a closed-loop ecosystem that competitors could not easily replicate. The iTunes Music Store launched in April 2003 with 200,000 tracks and the licensing cooperation of all four major labels — a coup Steve Jobs negotiated personally by arguing that legal digital sales would curb piracy without cannibalizing physical sales.

The iPod nano, introduced September 7, 2005, replaced the iPod mini at a thinner form factor and lower price point ($199 for 1GB), dramatically expanding the consumer base for portable digital music and accelerating iTunes purchases. By year-end, Apple had sold its one-billionth song — a milestone Jobs celebrated publicly, anchoring the platform's cultural legitimacy.

Competitors existed but struggled to match iTunes' execution: Napster had relaunched as a legal subscription service in 2003; Microsoft's MSN Music was available; RealNetworks operated Rhapsody; Sony operated Connect. None achieved more than 10% combined of legal digital sales in 2005.

## The Album-Unbundling Shift

The $0.99 single-track purchase model — championed by Steve Jobs over label executives who initially preferred album-only or higher pricing — represented a structural break with the post-LP music economy. Since the CD replaced vinyl in the 1980s, consumers had been compelled to purchase albums (typically 10–14 tracks at $15–18) to access individual hit songs. iTunes reversed this, enabling "unbundling": a consumer who wanted only one song from an album now paid $0.99 rather than $15.

Labels were initially wary, fearing that unbundling would cannibalize album revenue. This proved partially correct: the album format declined steadily throughout the 2000s and 2010s. However, the revenue per-unit from $0.99 digital singles proved far lower than the per-song revenue from album sales, compressing artist and label margins — a tension that would later drive the industry toward streaming models.

## Industry Response and Infrastructure Change

Physical music retailers began experiencing measurable declines in 2005. Tower Records, once the dominant U.S. music retail chain with over 200 stores and $1 billion in annual revenue, filed for bankruptcy protection in February 2004 and would liquidate entirely in October 2006. Independent record shops across North America and Europe saw declining foot traffic as consumers increasingly purchased music digitally rather than in stores.

The labels themselves undertook a costly and uneven digital licensing infrastructure build-out: creating digital delivery standards, metadata management systems, and rights clearance processes for individual track licensing — tasks far more granular than the album-unit licensing model they had operated under since the 1950s. New roles emerged in the industry: digital licensing managers, metadata curators, platform relations specialists. Many of these roles were created within existing label structures, while new digital distribution intermediaries (DistroKid's predecessors, TuneCore's antecedents) began forming to serve independent artists directly.

## DRM and Its Contradictions

All iTunes downloads in 2005 were protected by Apple's FairPlay Digital Rights Management (DRM) system, which restricted playback to Apple devices and a limited number of authorized computers. DRM was the compromise that had enabled Jobs to secure licensing from major labels skeptical of unprotected digital files. However, FairPlay created a structural lock-in: consumers who purchased iTunes music could not easily transfer their libraries to competing devices, reinforcing Apple's ecosystem dominance.

Consumer backlash against DRM restrictions and ongoing technical workarounds (stripping DRM via CD burning/reimporting) would eventually pressure the industry; Apple removed DRM from iTunes downloads in April 2009 following a negotiated deal with EMI and then the remaining major labels, making all iTunes music DRM-free at $1.29 per track.

## Long-term Impact

The 2005 digital music boom validated a fundamental principle of the digital economy: consumers would pay for convenient, legal digital content when priced reasonably and delivered seamlessly. This directly countered the post-Napster assumption that digital piracy had made paid digital content commercially unviable. iTunes' success provided a proof-of-concept that shaped digital distribution models across creative industries — for books (Kindle, launched 2007), video (Netflix streaming, launched 2007), and software (App Store, launched 2008).

The 2005 digital music revolution also set the stage for streaming, which would further displace downloads in the 2010s. Spotify, founded in 2006 and launched publicly in 2008, would apply the lessons of iTunes — legal, frictionless, artist-compensating — while replacing the per-download model with per-stream subscription revenue. By 2020, streaming accounted for 83% of U.S. music industry revenue, completing the transition that iTunes began in 2003 and accelerated in 2005.
