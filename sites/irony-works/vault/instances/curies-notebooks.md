# Curie's Notebooks

Curie carried radium vials in her pockets and admired their glow in desk drawers; the element she named for its rays wrote itself into everything she touched — the notebooks, the furniture, by some accounts her cookbooks. She died in 1934 of aplastic anemia, the discoverer consumed by the discovery, and was eventually reinterred in the Panthéon in a lead-lined coffin: honored by the nation, and simultaneously contained by it.

The notebooks earn the entry because they invert the *specific* expectation attached to archives. Paper is how science outlives its dangers — the reaction ends, the record remains. Hers is the rare archive where the record is the reaction: the data about radioactivity, still radioactive, will outlast every reader who signs the waiver to see it. The half-life of radium-226 means her handwriting stays hazardous roughly as long as English has existed. Scholarship with a dosimeter.
