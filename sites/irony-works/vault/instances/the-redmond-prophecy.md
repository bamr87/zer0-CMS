# The Redmond Prophecy

*The exhibit below was composed on 2026-09-14, in period voice. No such post exists in the 1995 archives. Every fact it jokes about is real; the post is not. The vault does not forge its evidence — it labels its fiction.*

Told straight: in August 1995 Microsoft shipped Windows 95 to "Start Me Up", lit the Empire State Building in its logo's colours, and was, by every account including its own, the opposite of free software. Its founder's first public act had been a 1976 letter informing hobbyists that most of them stole their software. Its revenue was the per-copy licence, the one thing free software by construction does not have. Linux was a four-year-old kernel on an FTP server in North Carolina; the phrase "open source" would not be coined for another two and a half years. The internet's favourite joke about Microsoft that year was the acquisition hoax — the previous December a spoof wire story had the company buying the Catholic Church, complete with a Redmond spokesman, and it travelled far enough that Microsoft issued a real denial. *Microsoft to become the world's largest free-software maintainer* was a headline in exactly that genre: funny because impossible. So here is the post that could have gone out that week, written with thirty-one years of hindsight and none of the vocabulary.

MICROSOFT TO BECOME WORLD'S LARGEST FREE SOFTWARE MAINTAINER

"Where do you want to go today?"  --  "/pub/Linux"

REDMOND, Wash., Aug. 24 (AP) -- Hours after "Start Me Up" played for
the last time and the last customer left the midnight line at the
Bellevue Egghead, Microsoft Corp. chairman Bill Gates announced that
the company will become, over the next quarter-century, the world's
largest maintainer of free software, as measured by whoever ends up
doing the counting.

"In 1976 I wrote that most hobbyists steal their software," Gates
told reporters, reading from the Open Letter to Hobbyists on a
Windows 95 laptop that had not yet crashed.  "We have decided to save
them the trouble."

Under the plan, the company that this week spent a reported $300
million teaching the public where the Start button is will:

  * Purchase sunsite.unc.edu and tsx-11.mit.edu, the two ftp sites
    where Linux lives, "so that the world's free software has a home
    it can never be evicted from, except by us."

  * Ship a complete copy of Linux inside a future version of
    Windows, "for compatibility with the future."  The kernel will be
    built in Redmond.  Source will be provided, as the licence
    requires, in a directory nobody looks in.

  * Submit more patches to Linus Torvalds than any other company on
    Earth, "until he accepts our hardware drivers."

  * Join Linux International as its largest dues-paying member, at
    whatever the top tier ends up being called.

  * Release the source code to MS-DOS the moment it becomes
    worthless, which the company estimates at around 2014.

  * Release the source code to the Windows Calculator, so that
    someone, somewhere, can finally make 3.11 minus 3.1 equal 0.01.

  * Give a future version of Windows away for nothing, on the theory
    that it is not the software you charge for.

  * Rebuild Internet Explorer on a browser engine the company did
    not write.  This will be the second browser Microsoft has not
    written, after the first.

  * Complete the GNU Hurd, which the Free Software Foundation has
    described as "coming soon" since 1990.  Redmond estimates
    delivery in the first quarter of 1996.

The company's lawyers, who have reviewed the GNU General Public
License, describe it as "fine, actually," and have offered
Microsoft's patents to anyone who would like them.

Employment offers have gone out to the author of Python, a free
scripting language named after a British comedy troupe, and to the
founders of whatever free desktop environment gets written next.

Richard Stallman could not be reached for comment.  Sources at the
Free Software Foundation say he was offered the title of Chief
Evangelist and countered with "GNU/Microsoft."

Linus Torvalds, reached at the University of Helsinki, said he had
not read the announcement and would not be merging it.

Chairman Gates was later photographed embracing a gnu.

REACTION

Netscape Communications Corp., whose shares have more than doubled
since its public offering two weeks ago, dismissed the announcement.
"What would we do -- give away our source code?" a spokesman said.

IBM said it would not spend a billion dollars on a hobbyist
operating system when it already has OS/2 Warp, and would certainly
not spend thirty-four.

Caldera Inc., a Utah company that sells Linux with a desktop on it,
said it would sooner sue every Unix user in America than take
Microsoft's money.

Apple Computer could not be reached.  A receptionist said the
company was busy looking for an operating system.

The Microsoft Network, which went live this morning, will continue
to run on Windows NT "for the foreseeable future," a spokesman said.
"We would never run our own service on free software."

Microsoft's public relations department declined to deny the report,
on the grounds that it had never heard of Linux.

Microsoft shares fell 3 1/8 on the news and recovered 4 1/4 on the
realization that it was a joke.  Analysts said the market would
revisit the question in about twenty years.

## What stayed a joke

Three gags did not come true, and they calibrate the entry. The source code to Windows has never been released — it has only leaked, parts of NT 4 and 2000 in 2004 and XP in 2020, which is a different genre. The GNU Hurd, which the parodist has Redmond finishing by the first quarter of 1996, has as of this planting still not reached a 1.0 release; the one joke that survived did so because its target kept it alive. And the headline word. Microsoft did not become the largest maintainer of *free software* in the sense a 1995 reader meant: its own projects go out under MIT and Apache licences, the copyleft code it distributes is other people's, and the Free Software Foundation would decline to count most of it. The concept had to be renamed first — in February 1998, by people who wanted, explicitly, a term that companies like Microsoft could say out loud — and only then could the prophecy be fulfilled to the letter. The parody used the only word it had, and by that word it is still false.

The gate's substitution test: is this ironic, or merely surprising? It is ironic because the reversal comments on the expectation. The 1995 reader took hostility to free software for Microsoft's nature. It was Microsoft's revenue. The per-copy licence was the whole quarrel — a zero price attacks exactly that line — and once the money moved to renting computers by the hour and to owning the place where developers work, Linux stopped being the competitor and became the workload. Microsoft was not converted; the ground the quarrel stood on was sold. That is why the reversal reads as composed rather than random, and why it scores on inevitability: every gag turns out to be the rational move of a company whose incentives were about to flip, written by someone who could see the incentives and not the flip. Nor is it hypocrisy, the gate's nearest neighbour — no rule was preached and broken; a worldly expectation, held by everyone including its subject, was inverted by the world.

The [[dramatic]] layer follows from the form of the exhibit. The 2026 reader is the audience; the 1995 poster is the character, confident on stage, and the confidence is the mechanism — every line detonates twice, once as a joke and once as a date. It is [[historical]] irony with the audience's seat left deliberately empty, so the reader has to take it. And the deadpan wire-story register, carrying an absurd proposition without one crack in the voice, is the [[a-modest-proposal]] method pointed at a press release: the register that made the Catholic Church hoax believable is the register that makes this one, in retrospect, the only accurate report of the week.
