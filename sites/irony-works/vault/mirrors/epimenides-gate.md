Epimenides of Crete is supposed to have said, sometime in the sixth century BCE, that Cretans are always liars. The line survives because Callimachus quoted it in his hymn to Zeus, mocking the Cretans for showing visitors the god's tomb, and because Saint Paul quoted it again in the letter to Titus and added, without visible irony, that the testimony is true — the most-printed book in the world endorsing a Cretan's claim that Cretans never tell the truth. Logic adopted the sentence as the Liar's ancestor. It is not the Liar. If the sentence is true, Epimenides is lying, so it is false; but if it is false, its negation is only that some Cretan sometimes tells the truth, which is a perfectly ordinary state of affairs in which Epimenides is lying as usual. The sentence is merely false. Prior (1958) drew out the odd residue — for it to be consistently false some other Cretan utterance must be true, so logic appears to prove a fact about Cretan speech — but oddness is not contradiction, and the gate scores collision, not oddness.

The gate's five axes are the [[the-anatomy-of-a-paradox|anatomy]] applied adversarially. **Plausibility**: does each premise, alone, command assent? **Validity**: does the inference fool a competent reader — a fallacy found in seconds scores nothing, one that hid for centuries scores full marks? **Collision**: is the conclusion genuinely unacceptable, and to what — an intuition, a fact, or logic? **Cost**: does resolving it cost something, or does it dissolve the moment a word is disambiguated? **Specificity**: the canonical formulation, the attribution, the dates, checked against the `veracity` claim. Seven of ten passes. Below that the candidate goes to the [[the-merely-puzzling|second ledger]] with a verdict that names the neighbour it turned out to be: merely surprising, an equivocation, a transparent fallacy, a dilemma, an anomaly, or not even wrong.

The gate's inaugural session ran its namesake through the rubric and composted it — five of ten, a transparent fallacy, the step from "false" to "therefore he speaks truly" being the visible trick — and then passed [[the-liar]], the sentence's strengthened descendant, at ten. That is the rubric working, and it is also, unavoidably, the joke: a standard named for the most famous example of the thing it judges, which fails. It is the same arrangement as the [[alanis-gate|Alanis Gate]] next door, where the song passes and its verses do not. The vault has now built two gates and named both for near misses. Anyone looking for a pattern should see [[the-unfeeling-curator]].
