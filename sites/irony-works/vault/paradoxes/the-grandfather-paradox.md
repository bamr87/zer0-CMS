# The Grandfather Paradox

The readers got there before the writers. Letters printed in *Amazing Stories* in 1927 and 1928 already asked what happens if a time traveller shoots his younger self, or goes back sixty years to kill a grandfather before a parent is conceived, and Hugo Gernsback answered them in a 1929 editorial; René Barjavel's *Le Voyageur imprudent* (1943) gave the scenario its ancestor and its name. The argument is short. Grant the traveller a working machine, a loaded gun, and a grandfather who has not yet met a grandmother. If the shot succeeds, the traveller was never born and never travelled, so the shot was never fired. If the shot was fired, the traveller exists, so it did not succeed. Whichever way the story goes, it contradicts the way it went.

It reads as an [[antinomy]] and is filed as [[falsidical]], because everyone agrees the conclusion is false and the dispute is entirely over which premise to bill. Lewis (1976) argued that there is no contradiction to resolve, only an equivocation in *can*: the traveller can kill the grandfather in the sense that nothing about the gun, the aim, or the moment prevents it, and cannot in the sense that the world already contains a grandchild — both true, relative to different sets of facts, in the way that Lewis could speak Finnish relative to the facts about his larynx and could not relative to the facts about his education. The shot is fired and misses, for some ordinary reason, because the one history that happened is the one in which it missed. Physics arrived at the same door from the other side. Gödel (1949) had shown that general relativity permits closed timelike curves — paths through spacetime that return to their own past — and Novikov's principle, stated formally by Friedman, Morris, Thorne and colleagues (1990), holds that on such a curve only self-consistent histories have nonzero probability: the standard illustration is a billiard ball sent back through a wormhole to knock its earlier self off course, which turns out always to arrive at an angle that produces exactly the nudge that sent it. Deutsch (1991) kept the successful shot and sent the traveller into a different branch of a quantum multiverse, at the price of the grandfather being someone else's. The cheapest resolution refuses the `hypothesis` altogether, and the entry names it so that the refusal is visible as a move rather than an assumption.

The paradox is the vault's clearest hypothetical: its literature is real and its premises are not. It stays `contested` because Lewis's dissolution and Novikov's constraint agree that the grandfather survives without agreeing on why, and because nobody has yet built the curve.
