# Newcomb's Problem

William Newcomb, a physicist at the Lawrence Livermore laboratory, devised the problem in 1960 and never published it; Robert Nozick learned of it by word of mouth, put it in print in a 1969 volume for Carl Hempel, and reported the feature that has defined it since: nearly everyone finds the answer obvious, and the room splits almost evenly on which answer that is. The setup is a game with a predictor. Box A holds a thousand dollars in plain view. Box B holds a million dollars or nothing, and the predictor filled it yesterday according to a forecast of what you will do now — a million if it forecast that you would take B alone, nothing if it forecast that you would take both. The predictor has never been wrong. You may take both boxes or B alone.

Two principles of rational choice, each of which decision theory endorses, now issue opposite instructions. Dominance says take both: the money in B is already there or already absent, and in either case two boxes beat one by a thousand dollars. Expected utility says take one: nearly everyone who took both walked away with a thousand, nearly everyone who took one walked away with a million, and you have the record. The two principles agree on every ordinary problem; Newcomb's is the case that separates them, which is why the vault files it as an [[antinomy]] of decision rather than a puzzle about money. The resolutions are revisions to the theory of rationality itself. Gibbard and Harper (1978) and Lewis (1981) built causal decision theory to say that expected utility must be computed over what your choice *brings about*, not what it is evidence of — your taking one box does not put the million in it — and two-boxing follows. Evidentialists reply that a theory of rationality that predictably leaves its adherents poorer has misunderstood the assignment. When Gardner ran the problem in *Scientific American* the mail favoured one box by five to two; in the 2020 PhilPapers survey professional philosophers leaned the other way, two-boxing by a plurality and never a majority. Lewis argued that the prisoners' dilemma is two Newcomb problems side by side, which if right means the paradox is not an exotic case but the ordinary one.

The entry carries a `hypothesis` because the whole argument rides on the predictor. A reliable forecaster of a choice not yet made is a counterfactual, and one live resolution is to refuse it — to argue that if the prediction is genuinely reliable your choice is not free, and if your choice is free the record is luck. That resolution costs the least logic and the most physics, and the vault holds the position without rooting for it.
