# The Fermi Paradox

In the summer of 1950, over lunch at Los Alamos, Enrico Fermi asked "Where is everybody?" — the recollections of Teller, York, and Konopinski, gathered by Eric Jones in 1985, agree on the question and disagree on nearly everything else, including what Fermi concluded. The argument that now carries his name was written down by Michael Hart in 1975. The galaxy is old and enormous; the Sun is a latecomer; if intelligence arises at all often and any civilisation ever builds ships or transmitters, then by simple multiplication the solar system should have been reached or hailed long before humans were there to notice. It has not been, so far as anyone can tell. Hart's conclusion was that we are alone; Brin (1983) called the silence great and catalogued the alternatives.

The vault files this as [[falsidical]] and `open`, and the two labels are doing different jobs. Falsidical because the conclusion collides with an observation and everyone agrees the observation wins: at least one premise is false. Open because the argument is symmetrical about which — each resolution in the literature is the denial of exactly one line. Rare Earth denies the second premise; Hanson's Great Filter (1998) denies it too but locates the rarity at a specific step, and asks, uncomfortably, whether the step is behind us or ahead; the zoo and dark-forest hypotheses deny the third, by making civilisations quiet on purpose; and Hart's own answer denies nothing, accepting that the premises hold and the galaxy is simply empty of anyone else. Sandberg, Drexler, and Ord (2018) took aim at the inference instead: the Drake-style multiplication that makes the conclusion feel forced treats point estimates as knowledge, and when each parameter is given the range the evidence actually supports, an empty observable universe comes out reasonably likely and the paradox, they argue, dissolves without any premise having to be false. Gray (2015) made the further case that the argument is neither Fermi's — his question was more likely about the feasibility of interstellar travel — nor, in the strict sense, a paradox at all, being an unanswered question rather than a contradiction.

Both objections are recorded here rather than adjudicated. The argument is Hart's and attested; the label is Fermi's only by folklore, and the vault keeps it because the literature does. What would settle the entry is a datum, not an argument. Until one arrives the fault stays `unlocated`, and a paradox whose fault is a research programme is the best reason the vault has a value called `open`.
