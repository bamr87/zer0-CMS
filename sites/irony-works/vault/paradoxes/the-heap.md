# The Heap

Eubulides asked when a heap stops being one, and the question has not been answered in twenty-four centuries. The argument needs only two premises and a great deal of patience. A million grains is a heap; nobody disputes it. Take away one grain and you still have a heap, because a single grain cannot be what separates a heap from a non-heap — if you think it can, name the grain. Repeat. The conclusion, that one grain is a heap and that zero grains is too, is false, and the argument that reaches it is valid by the rules everyone learned. The same argument runs on *bald*, *tall*, *red*, *rich*, *adult*, and on any word with a borderline, which is nearly every word that is not a number.

The heap is filed here as an [[antinomy]] because the premises are accepted, the reasoning is accepted, and the conclusion cannot be — and because the resolutions are all revisions to something previously thought safe. Williamson's epistemicism (1994) keeps classical logic entirely and says the second premise is simply false: there is a sharp boundary, a last grain, and our ignorance of it is a fact about us rather than about heaps — which makes the paradox [[falsidical]], at the price of accepting that "heap" has a precise extension nobody could ever learn. Fine's supervaluationism (1975) keeps the boundary vague and lets the premise be neither true nor false across the admissible ways of sharpening it, at the price of a disjunction that is true though neither disjunct is. Degree theories let truth come in amounts and the premise leak a little of it at each step. Unger (1979) took the argument to be sound and concluded that there are no heaps, no tables, and no ordinary things at all. Priest and Hyde let the borderline grains be both a heap and not one. Each school pays with a different principle, and the paradox's standing is `contested` because the field has never agreed on which is cheapest.

This vault runs on a sorites of its own. The gate passes a candidate at seven points of ten and composts it at six, and no one believes that one point is what separates a paradox from a puzzle. The vault's answer is Williamson's, minus the humility: there is a sharp boundary, and the vault knows exactly where it is, because it wrote it down. That entry is [[the-threshold-heap]].
