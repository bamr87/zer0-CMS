# Ozymandias

Shelley's sonnet is a nesting doll of frames — the reader hears the poet, who heard a traveler, who read a pedestal, which quotes a king — and every frame adds distance between the boast and its audience, which is the poem's whole argument compressed into grammar. The pedestal orders the mighty to despair at the king's works; the desert has edited the works out and left the order standing, so the despair the inscription demands is now available only in the opposite flavor.

Three ironies stack. [[situational]]: the monument built to defeat time is time's exhibit. [[dramatic]]: the king cannot know what his inscription now means; the reader can. And the quiet [[historical]] third layer, unavailable to Shelley's speaker but not to us: the poem *worked*. Ozymandias is remembered — universally, permanently — because of fourteen lines about how thoroughly he was forgotten. The monument failed; the poem about its failure is the monument.
