# A Modest Proposal

Swift's 1729 pamphlet is the endurance record for [[structural]] irony: the mask never slips. The narrator is helpful, numerate, mildly self-congratulatory — he has done the arithmetic on infants as livestock and is quietly pleased with the margins. The horror is delivered entirely by tone-content mismatch, which is why the piece still gets cited by readers who miss it; a sufficiently committed deadpan is indistinguishable, to some audiences, from sincerity. That failure mode now has a name — Poe's law — and Swift hit it 250 years before the internet did.

The deeper stroke: the proposal's *actual* targets are the sober economic pamphlets it impersonates. Swift doesn't argue that treating the poor as stock is monstrous; he demonstrates that the era's respectable policy voice *already did*, and merely lacked the honesty of a recipe.
