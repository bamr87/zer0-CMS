# Veridical Paradox

**Definition.** A paradox whose conclusion is true: the argument is sound, the surprise is the whole event, and what gives way is the intuition that said it could not be so.

**Mechanism.** Quine's first way (*The Ways of Paradox*, 1962). The premises hold, the inference holds, and the conclusion holds — which is why a veridical paradox leaves an argument standing and an intuition on the floor. Frederic in *The Pirates of Penzance* is twenty-one years old and has had five birthdays; the resolution (he was born on the twenty-ninth of February) adds no premise and removes none. The barber who shaves all and only those who do not shave themselves is Quine's other example: the argument validly proves that no such barber exists, and the only casualty is the assumption that one could. In this vault the `collision` field of a veridical paradox names an *intuition* — never a fact and never logic — and the `fault` field names the intuition that has to be retrained.

**Commonly confused with.** Mere surprise. A counterintuitive fact is not a paradox until an argument carries a reasonable reader from premises they accept to a conclusion they refuse; the birthday problem earns the name because the reader's own arithmetic does the carrying, while the so-called paradox of choice is a finding with a headline. The test: can the premises be listed, and does a competent reader balk only at the last line? If there is no last line, it is [[the-merely-puzzling|merely surprising]].
