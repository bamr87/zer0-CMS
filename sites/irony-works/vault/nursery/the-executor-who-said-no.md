# The Executor Who Said No

There are two notes. The first, found in Kafka's desk after his death in June 1924, is absolute: everything left behind — notebooks, manuscripts, letters — "is to be burned unread and to the last page." The second, in pencil, is a partial amnesty, sparing six works Kafka could bear to have survive him: *The Judgment*, *The Stoker*, *Metamorphosis*, *In the Penal Colony*, *A Country Doctor*, *A Hunger Artist*. Everything else was to go, including three unfinished novels no one had read.

The detail that turns this from a broken promise into an irony is dated 1921. Kafka had shown Brod the instruction three years earlier, while alive and able to choose otherwise, and Brod had told him flatly that he would never carry it out. Kafka then left his literary estate to that same man, and changed neither the executor nor the instruction. Brod published *The Trial* in 1925, *The Castle* in 1926, *Amerika* in 1927, and spent the rest of his life explaining himself — most durably in the postscript where he recorded his own advance refusal, entering into evidence the fact that makes his disobedience look less like betrayal than like casting.

What the reversal comments on is the authority of an author over his own meaning. Kafka's verdict on the work was that it was failure; posterity's verdict, available only because the verdict was ignored, is that it was the century. Brod, who wrote his own novels steadily and is read for none of them, is remembered exclusively for the one literary judgment in which he overruled a better writer — and was right. The estate's later decades in court, argued over by parties Kafka never met, are the same irony compounding interest: the papers he wanted unread became property worth litigating.
