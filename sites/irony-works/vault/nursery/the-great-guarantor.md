The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics adopted its second constitution on December 5, 1936. Drafted substantially by Nikolai Bukharin — who would himself be shot for treason twenty-six months later, after a show trial — it guaranteed freedom of speech, freedom of the press, freedom of assembly and demonstration, and the inviolability of the person. Western observers who had spent a decade mediating their skepticism of Soviet claims found in it something that looked like evidence: a state codifying limits on itself. The document was taken seriously enough abroad that comparisons to the United States Bill of Rights were not considered absurd.

Within weeks of its ratification, Stalin opened what would become the Great Purge. Between 1936 and 1938, approximately 750,000 people were shot by the NKVD and somewhere between one and two million more were consigned to the Gulag — the very citizens whose freedoms the constitution had just enumerated. What makes this more than coincidence of timing is that the constitution was not merely silent while the terror proceeded; it was procedurally present. Show trials were conducted under the rubric of "socialist legality," a phrase drawn directly from the constitutional order. Courts created by the constitution sentenced defendants under laws the constitution legitimated. The guarantee did not simply fail to protect — it supplied the vocabulary through which protection's annihilation was formalized. Bukharin's own trial was a performance of socialist legality. The document he helped write was the stage.

The veracity of the concurrent timeline is not seriously disputed: Getty, Conquest, and Kotkin establish it from independent archival directions. What historians contest is the interpretive question — whether the constitution was cynical theater from conception, or whether Stalin believed in some version of it while simultaneously authorizing mass killing, without experiencing the two as contradictory. Getty's revisionist reading suggests the purge was partly a chaotic escalation rather than a scripted performance, which makes the irony stranger rather than smaller: a state that could draft rights with apparent sincerity while possessing no institutional capacity to honor them, and possibly no clear awareness of the gap. Calculated hypocrisy would at least require knowing what the words meant.
