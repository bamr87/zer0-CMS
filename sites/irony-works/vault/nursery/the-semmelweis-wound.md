# The Semmelweis Wound

In 1847, at Vienna General Hospital, Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that the maternity ward staffed by physicians — who arrived at deliveries straight from the autopsy room — killed mothers at several times the rate of the ward staffed by midwives, who did not. His prescription was almost insultingly simple: wash hands in chlorinated lime. Mortality collapsed from roughly ten percent toward one. The profession's response was not gratitude but offense — the suggestion that a gentleman's hands could carry death was received as slander — and Semmelweis was eased out of Vienna, then out of patience, and finally, in 1865, into the Döbling asylum, committed with the cooperation of his colleagues.

He died there fourteen days later, aged forty-seven, of pyaemia from a gangrenous wound, most likely inflicted by asylum guards. Pyaemia is septicemia: the same class of infection as the childbed fever he had spent his career preventing. The reversal is not that a doctor died of disease — doctors do. It is that medicine, offered the cure, chose instead to destroy the man carrying it, and the destruction was denominated in the currency of his own discovery. The refusal to believe that hands could kill is what killed him.

The autopsy and cause of death are documented; the beating is attested by twentieth-century examinations of his remains, though the wound's exact origin is less certain than the sepsis that finished it. The parallel requires no sharpening either way.
