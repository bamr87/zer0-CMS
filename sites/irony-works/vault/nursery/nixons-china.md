Richard Nixon spent twenty years building the most unambiguous anti-communist record in American politics. As a freshman congressman in 1947 he joined the House Un-American Activities Committee; his pursuit of Alger Hiss through 1950 made him a national figure; his Senate campaign that year against Helen Gahagan Douglas — whom he branded "the Pink Lady" — cemented the identity. By the time Dwight Eisenhower chose him as a running mate in 1952, Nixon's name had become a synonym for vigilance against communist infiltration. He carried that credential through two terms as Vice President and into the 1960 and 1968 presidential campaigns. No postwar politician had done more to make anti-communism the test of American patriotism.

In February 1972, Nixon flew to Beijing and shook Mao Zedong's hand. The diplomatic opening that followed — nearly a quarter-century after the People's Republic replaced the Republic of China as the mainland government — could not have been accomplished by any other plausible American president. A Democrat would have been savaged as soft; a moderate Republican accused of appeasement; a liberal hawk would have spent his political capital before the plane touched down. Nixon's extremity was not a liability to the mission — it was the credential the mission required. The man who had defined anti-communism in postwar America was structurally the only man immune to the charge of surrendering to it. His career's most defining posture had, by strict political logic, become its own instrument of undoing.

The visit produced the Shanghai Communiqué and a generation of normalized relations. The phrase "only Nixon can go to China" entered political vocabulary as a general axiom for the paradox — the logic applies wherever prior extreme positioning grants unique credibility to cross a line that would destroy anyone with less history on the other side. The veracity of the diplomatic history is solid; the precise origination of the phrase is murkier, with Saul Friedman and others cited, and *Star Trek VI* giving it its most durable pop-cultural form.
