The request was one line: *Microsoft to become the largest open sourced maintainer. A parody written in 1995. Write it.* The deliverable is a vault entry in Irony Works called [The Redmond Prophecy](https://github.com/bamr87/irony-works/blob/main/vault/instances/the-redmond-prophecy.md): a spoof Usenet post, dated the day after Windows 95 shipped, in which Microsoft announces it will become the world's largest maintainer of free software — followed by a ledger showing that every gag in it came true between 2009 and 2024. The reusable part is not the jokes. It is the method for writing satire that has to survive a fact-checker, and the three places the tooling pushed back.

## Write the joke backwards from the record

A parody written in 2026 and set in 1995 is dramatic irony with the audience's seat left empty: the reader knows the ending, the narrator does not. That only works if the ending is real. So the first pass was not comedy at all — it was a list of what Microsoft actually did, with dates: the 20,000-line Hyper-V driver submission (July 2009), the LWN statistic that made it the top contributor to Linux 3.0 by changesets (July 2011), the "Microsoft ♥ Linux" slide (October 2014), .NET (November 2014), Visual Studio Code (November 2015), the Windows Subsystem for Linux (March 2016), the Linux Foundation Platinum seat (November 2016), GitHub for $7.5 billion (June 2018), the Open Invention Network pledge (October 2018), Edge on Chromium (December 2018), the Calculator's source (March 2019), Guido van Rossum's hire (November 2020), MS-DOS 4.0 under MIT (April 2024). Then each fact was rewritten as the thing that would have sounded absurd in August 1995. *Ship a complete Linux inside Windows* is funnier than anything invented, because it is a press release from 2019 read out loud in a room that still has Windows 3.1 in it.

The ledger that resulted is in the entry as a two-column table, gag on the left and record on the right. It doubles as the source list, and it is the part the vault's adversarial gate actually scores: the rubric's *specificity* axis wants names, dates, and verifiability, and a table of them is harder to argue with than a paragraph. **When AI drafts satire, ask for the facts first and the jokes second**, and keep the facts in a structure you can check row by row.

## Audit every noun against the date

A hard date is a vocabulary budget. The phrase "open source" did not exist in 1995 — Christine Peterson proposed it in February 1998, twelve days after Netscape announced it would release its browser code — so the parody had to say "free software," which changed the joke: Microsoft did become the largest maintainer of *open source* and did not become the largest maintainer of *free software* in the Free Software Foundation's sense, and the entry got a whole section out of the fact that the concept had to be renamed before the prophecy could come true. Tux did not exist either (1996), so the punchline is Gates embracing a gnu. KDE (October 1996) and GNOME (1997) could not be named, so the parody hires "the founders of whatever free desktop environment gets written next" — which is exactly what happened, and reads better for being vague. The Linux Foundation did not exist; Linux International did. Slashdot, Google, GitHub: out. The Halloween Documents, the "Linux is a cancer" line: three and six years in the future, so they live in the commentary, not the exhibit.

The audit is a prompt pattern. *List every proper noun, product, slogan, and term of art you want to use, with the year it entered use, and strike anything after the setting.* A model will happily supply the years, and it is much better at checking a list than at noticing an anachronism mid-sentence. The date also gives you period texture for free once you ask for it: the $300 million launch campaign, the midnight lines at Egghead, the Windows 3.1 Calculator that displayed 3.11 minus 3.1 as 0.00, kernel 1.2.13 fitting on a floppy, `anon.penet.fi` as the anonymous remailer of choice in the year before it closed.

## Leave something unfulfilled

A prophecy in which everything comes true is a listicle. Three gags were left standing on purpose: the source to Windows, which has only ever leaked; the GNU Hurd, which the parodist has Redmond finishing by early 1996 and which still has no 1.0 release; and the word "free" itself. They are the calibration set, in the same sense the vault's gates carry one — the rows that prove the scoring is not rigged. If you are using a model to write hindsight satire, ask it explicitly for the predictions that would *not* have come true. It will otherwise round every joke toward the punchline, because that is what the form rewards.

## Make the fake document internally honest

The exhibit is a Usenet post with headers, and the headers are period-correct in the ways that cost nothing: an INN-style `Message-ID`, a `Path:` through `news.funet.fi`, a crosspost to `comp.os.ms-windows.advocacy` for provocation, a `.sig` with the 640K line marked *attributed, denied, repeated*. The `Lines:` header — the body line count a 1995 newsreader would have filled in — was computed by a five-line `awk` pipeline over the finished text rather than typed. It is a small thing, but a forged document with a wrong line count is a forged document that knows it is one. The body is hard-wrapped at 72 columns because that is what the medium looked like, and it is inside a fenced code block because the vault's publishing script rewrites `[[wikilinks]]` with a regular expression that does not know what a code fence is. That is a real constraint: no double brackets inside exhibits, ever, or the pressing will link them.

## Label the fiction where the schema can see it

Irony Works runs on a `veracity` field — `attested`, `contested`, `legend`, `speculative` — and the founding rule is *never sharpen a story past its evidence*. A period-voice parody is the exact failure mode that rule exists for: it is a document that looks found and is not. So the entry says so in its first line, in italics, before the exhibit: composed on this date, no such post exists in the archives, every fact is real and the post is not. The `veracity` stays `attested`, because the irony being catalogued is Microsoft's real reversal, and the exhibit is the illustration. **If a model writes you a document in someone else's decade, make the label part of the artifact**, not part of the chat. Chats are not what gets published.

## Three things YAML and Liquid will do to you

The entry's frontmatter failed to parse on the first try, and the reasons are worth keeping. A YAML list item that *begins* with a double quote — `- "Microsoft Bids to Acquire Catholic Church", spoof AP wire story…` — is read as a quoted scalar followed by garbage, so any citation whose title comes first needs a leading word. A plain scalar cannot contain colon-space, which rules out the natural way of citing a headline like *ZDNet: Linux usage on Azure…*; an em-dash does the job. And because Jekyll renders the vault's collection through Liquid, anything that looks like `{{` or `{%` in an entry body is template syntax, not text — nothing in this piece tripped it, but a parody about software is exactly the kind of prose that eventually will. A one-line `js-yaml` parse check on the frontmatter, run before the publishing script, catches the first two before the CI does.

## The second pass: the human picks the headline

The first draft had the strongest fact in the ledger and nowhere else. Microsoft does not merely contribute to open source; it owns the venue — GitHub, where the canonical source of Python, Rust, Node.js, Kubernetes, LLVM, PyTorch and, since 2025, Firefox is kept — and npm, the largest package registry in the world, and the most used editor (Visual Studio Code, just under three-quarters of Stack Overflow's respondents), and a language that GitHub's own 2025 count put first on the platform. The maintainer's steer was one sentence: *highlight the fact that Microsoft owns GitHub and now the most popular open source tools and repos.* That sentence moved the fact from a table row into the `reversal` field, which is the text the site's cards show, and into a section of its own with three new gags in the exhibit to carry it (the CD-ROM distributor, the editor that beats Emacs and vi put together, the language that ranks on the ftp site it just bought). A model distributes facts evenly, because evenness is what a ledger rewards. Deciding which fact is the headline is editorial judgment, and it took a human one line to supply it. **Leave room in the workflow for that line**: a draft you can re-weight cheaply is worth more than a draft that is finished.

The re-weighting also produced the entry's most useful caveat. Saying "Microsoft owns the most popular open-source editor" is true of the source and not of the binary — Microsoft's own VS Code builds carry a proprietary licence, which is why VSCodium exists — and the ledger row now says so. Emphasis is exactly where a vault that runs on veracity is most tempted to round up; the fix is to let the row carry its own asterisk.

## Using AI for this

Three habits carried the session. Verify by table, not by vibe: the model's memory of dates was good and was still checked row by row, with the rows kept in the artifact. Constrain by date, then ask for the vocabulary audit as its own step, because the constraint is what produced the best joke in the piece. And point the project's own adversary at the result — the Alanis Gate scores this entry on the pull request, and the entry was written against its five axes on purpose: a genuine expectation genuinely inverted, a reversal that comments on the expectation (the money left the licence; Redmond was not converted), hindsight-coherence, dates, and a paragraph arguing why it is not the neighbour called hypocrisy. Writing to the rubric is not gaming it. It is what the rubric is for.
