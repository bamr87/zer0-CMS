## Chapter 2: The Deal No One Understood

Kareem’s phone buzzed at 3:17 a.m., which was never a good sign. He squinted at the screen, half-expecting a family emergency from the old country—perhaps another worried letter about the failing crops or the strange taste of the water—but instead found a calendar invite titled: "URGENT: International Order Review." The sender was someone named "Chad" from "Global Strategic Initiatives," which sounded less like a department and more like a Bond villain’s shell company, the kind that might feature in a "private equity file."

By 8:00 a.m., Kareem was in a windowless conference room, surrounded by people who used words like "synergy," "goodwill," and "valuation" without irony, the same terms he’d heard whispered before his last day at the plant. The air was thick with the scent of burnt coffee and the unspoken fear that someone would ask a technical question about the very systems whose backups Kareem knew were a treasure trove of unexamined data.

A PowerPoint flickered to life. The deal: a massive, expedited order for custom equipment, destination: his homeland, a country whose name most of the board pronounced differently every time. The client was a government-linked conglomerate, Al-Madina Holdings, and the specs were so vague they might as well have been written in invisible ink, hinting at uses far beyond civilian infrastructure—uses that might explain the "dirt in the water" his family had mentioned.

Kareem, as the resident systems whisperer, was asked to explain why the customer was willing to pay double for a rush job. He considered suggesting that maybe the client just really loved American manufacturing, or perhaps that the new CEO, the former CFO with a reputation for aggressive financial engineering, had structured the deal in a way that benefited certain parties more than others. He decided sarcasm was a luxury for people with job security.

Instead, he pointed out the inconsistencies: the specs didn’t match the usual requirements, the payment terms were unusually generous, and the client’s project manager had a LinkedIn profile that listed "discretion" as a skill. The room went quiet, except for the CFO—soon to be the new CEO after the boardroom coup—who smiled like a man who’d just found a twenty in a dead man’s wallet and was about to announce a significant "impairment" to cover his tracks.

After the meeting, Kareem’s boss pulled him aside. “Just get it done, Kareem. Don’t ask too many questions. This is part of the new era.”

He got it done. The equipment shipped out on a foggy Tuesday, loaded onto trucks by workers who joked about sending American know-how overseas, unaware they were sealing their own layoff letters. Kareem watched the convoy leave, feeling a strange mix of pride and unease. He wondered if he’d just helped build something important, or if he’d just signed the warranty on his own obsolescence and contributed to a "country on fire."

That night, he called his cousin back home, the same cousin who would later send desperate "letters from home" about the unfolding crisis. “You ever hear of Al-Madina Holdings?” he asked. There was a pause. “Careful, Kareem. Those people don’t buy things—they buy people. This isn\'t just a meeting; it\'s an act of will with far-reaching consequences.”

Kareem laughed it off, but the next morning, he found a fruit basket on his desk. No card. Just a note: “Thanks for your discretion.” A token, perhaps, for his unwitting role in the "goodwill" that would soon be written off.

He stared at the fruit basket for a long time, then put it in the break room. Let someone else eat the poisoned apples.

He lingered by the window, watching the city wake up, the skyline fractured by the rising sun. The boardroom’s fluorescent lights flickered in his memory, and for the first time, Kareem wondered if he was part of something he couldn’t control, a complex scheme that would culminate in a devastating "impairment." The fruit basket sat untouched, a silent warning. Maybe the real product wasn’t the equipment, but him—a pawn in a game whose rules were hidden in "the private equity file" and whose outcome would be an "act of will" with global repercussions.
